## Text-based summary: March 31 glucose-starvation presentation

**Presentation title:** *Modeling Ploidy-Dependent Responses to Glucose Starvation* 

The presentation asks whether whole-genome doubling (WGD) changes how cancer cells respond to glucose limitation. The central idea is that WGD produces high-ploidy cells with roughly doubled chromosome content, and possibly larger size and altered energy demands. Because WGD is common in cancer and often associated with poor outcomes, the project investigates whether high-ploidy cells have distinct metabolic vulnerabilities or advantages under starvation.

### 1. Biological motivation

The deck begins by framing WGD as both a normal developmental phenomenon and an oncogenic process. In cancer, WGD is common, occurring in roughly one third of tumors, though incidence varies strongly by cancer type. WGD is generally presented as clinically unfavorable: it is associated in many cancer subtypes with treatment resistance, metastasis, cachexia, relapse, and poor survival.

However, WGD is not purely detrimental to cells. The presentation notes possible benefits, including greater robustness to deleterious mutations or aneuploidy and the opportunity for additional DNA to undergo neofunctionalization. It also highlights drawbacks: more chromosomes make mitosis harder, and WGD cells may be more sensitive to mitotic spindle assembly inhibitors.

### 2. Main question and hypothesis

The central question is:

**Do WGD or high-ploidy cells require more energy than diploid cells?**

The deck contrasts two scenarios. In one, only DNA quantity changes, so the extra burden might be limited to synthesizing and maintaining additional DNA. In another, “everything doubles,” implying approximately twofold energy use and additional scaling constraints such as altered surface-area-to-volume relationships.

The working hypothesis is that **high-ploidy cells need more energy than diploid cells**. The strategy is to induce WGD in five cell lines, perform in vitro glucose-starvation experiments, and model the resulting growth, death, and glucose-use dynamics.

### 3. Experimental design

The glucose-sensitivity assay exposes cells to variable starting glucose concentrations. Media are not refreshed, cells are imaged every 8 hours over 5 days, and media glucose is measured daily.

The observed qualitative behavior is simple: cells grow while glucose is available and die when glucose runs out. The data include live-cell counts and glucose measurements across multiple glucose conditions, ploidy states, and cell lines.

The image-analysis workflow uses Cellpose for segmentation, crops individual cells, generates vector embeddings with ResNet-18, and classifies cells. The presentation reports that this pipeline is consistent with manual alive-cell counting.

### 4. Modeling framework

The first model is a basic glucose-only consumer-resource model. Glucose uptake follows a saturating function:

**u = vr / (K + r)**

Cell growth is modeled as:

**g = yu − m**

where **y** is yield and **m** is a maintenance cost or death rate in the absence of glucose. Population growth and glucose depletion are then described by coupled equations for cell number and resource concentration.

The presentation argues that this simple model is insufficient because cells can die even when glucose remains abundant. This suggests either another limiting resource, accumulation of toxic waste, or both.

The modeling framework is therefore expanded to include:

**Two-resource models**

* Additive payoff: resources contribute to the same effective growth process.
* Limiting-resource payoff: growth is limited by the scarcest required component.
* Full pathway model: resources can be allocated to energy and precursor production.

**Energy versus precursor distinction**

* Glucose used for energy is fully catabolized and yields high ATP but leaves little biomass.
* Glucose used for precursors is diverted into biosynthesis, providing biomass but lower energy yield.

**Waste models**

* Cells may produce inhibitory waste proportional to resource uptake.
* Waste can either subtract directly from growth or inhibit growth through a saturating term.

In total, the analysis tests **12 consumer-resource models**: four resource schemas crossed with two waste schemas plus no-waste versions.

### 5. Hierarchical ploidy-effect model

The model generalizes across cell lines by giving each cell line its own baseline parameter set and estimating a shared ploidy effect. High-ploidy parameters are modeled as baseline cell-line parameters plus a ploidy-dependent shift.

This structure asks whether there is a common ploidy effect across different biological backgrounds rather than fitting each cell line independently.

### 6. Model-fitting results

Adding additional resources or waste significantly improves fit quality relative to the glucose-only model.

Across several cell-line plots, the presentation highlights recurring patterns:

**High-ploidy cells often do better in zero glucose.**
This is surprising under the original hypothesis that high-ploidy cells should simply require more energy.

**High-ploidy cells often deplete glucose faster.**
This suggests increased glucose consumption or uptake.

**Simple models cannot fully describe the data.**
More complex models appear to trade off explanations involving faster depletion, altered waste accumulation, and additional limiting resources.

**Some cell lines are inconsistent or outlying.**
SNU668 is described as difficult for many models. SUM-159-fuse is also flagged as unusual, both biologically and experimentally.

### 7. Inferred ploidy effects

The posterior distributions of shared ploidy effects suggest that models broadly agree on several parameter-level trends:

* **Ploidy increases glucose consumption.**
* **The effect of ploidy on uptake sensitivity is unclear.**
* **Ploidy decreases yield from glucose.**
* **Ploidy decreases apparent maintenance cost or death in the absence of glucose.**

The deck interprets the agreement across models as evidence of a real biological signal, even though the exact mechanistic interpretation remains uncertain.

### 8. Predicted ecological consequence

A major modeling prediction is that **resource starvation may select for high ploidy**.

The heatmaps compare predicted growth differences between high- and low-ploidy variants. Under scarce-resource conditions, high ploidy is predicted to win, apparently driven by the lower inferred maintenance-cost parameter. Under abundant-resource conditions, low ploidy is generally favored.

The presentation notes that competition experiments are being planned to test these predictions experimentally.

### 9. Generalizability and transfer tests

The deck then asks whether the learned ploidy effect is robust and generalizable.

A prediction/transfer test holds out one set of glucose experiments, re-estimates parameters, and asks whether the learned ploidy effect helps predict the held-out data. The conclusion is negative:

**The learned ploidy effect does not help predict new data well.**

The models are described as uniformly bad in this transfer setting, with partial success for MCF10A and particularly poor performance for SUM-159-fuse.

A separate stability test asks whether the learned ploidy effect is driven by one cell line. The conclusion is more reassuring:

**The learned ploidy effect is not driven by a single cell line.**

Even when individual lines are omitted, the same broad trends remain: yield decreases, uptake increases, and maintenance cost decreases with ploidy.

### 10. SUM-159-fuse as an outlier

SUM-159-fuse is highlighted as an outlier in two ways.

First, it is an outlier in cell size, suggesting that the relevant predictor may not be ploidy itself but cell volume. The deck suggests testing whether measured cell volume is a better predictor than ploidy.

Second, SUM-159-fuse differs in experimental protocol. Batch 1 used a censored glucose probe and no prestarvation phase, while Batch 2 used a glucose luminescence assay and included prestarvation. The presentation notes that current models treat cells as stateless, but the data suggest that internal cell state may matter.

### 11. Conclusions and next directions

The main conclusion is:

**Resource scarcity appears to favor high ploidy.**

The proposed next steps are:

1. Test the model predictions in competition experiments.
2. Identify additional limiting resources or waste products.
3. Develop stateful cell models.
4. Explicitly incorporate cell size rather than treating ploidy as the only scaling variable.

The final feedback slide also suggests interpreting results through biological differences among cell lines, such as MCF10A being non-cancer and potentially less glycolytic, and checking how robust the conclusions are to the observation model.

### Overall takeaway

The presentation starts from the expectation that high-ploidy cells may be energetically costly, but the data and models point to a more nuanced result. High-ploidy cells appear to consume glucose faster and have lower glucose yield, yet they may survive or perform better under glucose scarcity because their inferred maintenance cost is lower. This creates a counterintuitive prediction: **starvation may select for high-ploidy cells rather than against them**. The strongest unresolved issues are model transferability, hidden resources or wastes, cell-state effects, and whether cell volume is a better explanatory variable than ploidy alone.
