section_id: abstract
title: Abstract
---

## Manuscript Text

Ploidy changes cell size, resource demand, and stress tolerance, but its net ecological effect under nutrient limitation remains unresolved. We combined longitudinal image-derived live- and dead-cell counts, calibrated extracellular-glucose trajectories, direct trajectory features, hierarchical consumer-resource ordinary differential equation models, posterior parameter summaries, fitted-model transfer benchmarks, and mixed-state simulations across matched lower- and higher-ploidy states in five cell-line or derivation groups. Across these evidence layers, ploidy modulates response to glucose starvation. At 0 mM glucose, high-ploidy cultures accumulated more live-cell hours in all five groups, showing directly that high-ploidy cells are more robust to starvation. High ploidy increases glucose consumption rate: in the four-line primary comparison, intercept-bearing regressions of glucose-concentration drawdown on live-cell area under the curve had higher slopes in high-ploidy cultures. This culture-level glucose-concentration drawdown per live-cell hour is neither absolute glucose amount per cell-hour nor direct uptake or metabolic flux, and SUM-159-fuse reversed the shared direction. Within an operational five-model set admitted by an arbitrary optimized-target filter, high ploidy recurrently shifted apparent uptake upward and maintenance/death downward across four lines. High ploidy decreases yield per cell, but this is a qualified model-supported conclusion because observed low-glucose cell output was not normalized by cell size and fitted apparent yield changed direction among retained model structures. Cell size is proportional to ploidy as a general cross-line pattern, although SUM-159-fuse uncoupled higher ploidy from larger size. The claim that high ploidy increases volumetric yield remains an explicit working hypothesis, not an established result, because size-adjusted and volume-proxy-adjusted yield estimates remained mixed.

Arbitrary-context transfer of fitted ploidy effects to a held-out opposite-ploidy state remained unreliable. In a narrower fitted-model benchmark, environments learned after excluding one of 10 cell-line/ploidy states were frozen and assessed against a target-inclusive 10-of-10 fitted reference. Model-native searches retained the requested high-ploidy direction in 35 of 50 fixed tasks under both exact and robust criteria, whereas improvement for the requested low-ploidy direction was modest. These fixed-task fractions are descriptive, lack inferential interpretation, and varied with objective, search mode, model family, and target line. The benchmark therefore identifies sign retention in selected fitted families within the retained operational set, not prospective validation against an independently measured outcome, and qualifies rather than reverses the earlier transportability limitation. Finally, within this retained set, best-fit mixed-state simulations predict that starvation selects for high ploidy, whereas sustained abundance selects for low ploidy. These are schedule- and model-supported predictions rather than direct competition evidence; cellular-context heterogeneity was substantial, and the abundance prediction weakened in two other retained models when SUM-159-fuse was included. Agreement within this arbitrarily filtered set does not establish robustness across the full candidate model family.

title: Introduction

## Manuscript Text

Genome doubling and other high-ploidy states are common in cancer, but their fitness consequences depend on environment. Higher-ploidy cells are generally larger and must sustain more cellular material, creating potential costs to biosynthesis and resource use. Yet high ploidy can also alter stress tolerance. Under glucose limitation, these effects pose a specific ecological tradeoff: a state that persists better through resource depletion may nevertheless consume more resource per unit live-cell time or produce fewer cells, whereas low ploidy may gain an advantage when glucose remains abundant. Because ploidy, cell size, and starvation response are all influenced by cellular background and derivation history, this tradeoff should vary rather than define a universal high- or low-ploidy advantage.

Testing this idea requires separating observation from inference. Time-resolved live- and dead-cell counts and extracellular-glucose concentrations directly describe culture behavior. Features derived from those measurements can quantify growth, live-cell persistence, cell output, and glucose drawdown relative to accumulated live-cell time. In particular, the slope of glucose-concentration drawdown against live-cell area under the curve is a culture-level drawdown per live-cell hour; it is not an absolute glucose amount per cell-hour, a direct single-cell uptake measurement, or metabolic flux. Mechanistic models add a distinct interpretive layer by estimating apparent per-cell uptake, yield, glucose-response, and maintenance/death parameters and by introducing hidden resource or waste states. These fitted parameters and latent states can suggest mechanisms, but they remain conditional on model structure and cannot be equated with directly measured biochemical quantities. Simulations based on the fitted monoculture models add a final ecological layer: they predict how low- and high-ploidy populations would change in mixed culture under specified resource schedules rather than reporting observed competition.

Here we apply this layered framework to matched lower- and higher-ploidy states from five cell-line or derivation groups across a broad glucose series. We first establish measurement quality and ask how ploidy relates to cell and nuclear size. We then extract trajectory features to determine whether ploidy changes growth, starvation persistence, glucose drawdown per live-cell hour, and cell output without assigning those effects to a mechanism. We compare a family of consumer-resource models to test whether measured glucose alone is sufficient and to identify ploidy effects that recur across plausible model structures. Within this model layer, we distinguish transfer of fitted ploidy effects to arbitrary held-out contexts from the narrower question of whether an environment learned to favor high or low ploidy retains that direction for a target line in a target-inclusive fitted-model reference. Finally, we use fitted mixed-state simulations to ask whether resource depletion and sustained abundance support opposite selection directions, while testing sensitivity to cellular background, model choice, and initial conditions.

Across these layers, ploidy modulates the response to glucose starvation, but the effect is context-dependent. Cell size generally increased with ploidy, with a marked derivation-specific exception. High-ploidy cultures maintained more live-cell hours without supplied glucose across the full panel, supporting greater starvation robustness. In the primary four-group comparison, high ploidy also increased culture-level glucose drawdown per live-cell hour; the fifth derivation showed the opposite ordering for this derived coefficient. Within an operational five-model set admitted by an arbitrary optimized-target filter, mechanistic models recurrently inferred higher apparent uptake and lower maintenance/death for high ploidy, superimposed on larger cell-line-specific backgrounds, whereas apparent per-cell yield depended more strongly on retained model structure. Evidence for a high-ploidy per-cell yield cost is therefore model-supported but not structurally universal, and the possibility that high ploidy increases volumetric yield after accounting for cell size remains a working hypothesis. Fitted ploidy effects transferred unreliably across arbitrary held-out contexts, whereas selected model-native environments retained the requested direction in some retained model families and targets, especially for environments favoring high ploidy. This narrower benchmark is a descriptive comparison against a target-inclusive fitted-model reference, not prospective validation against an independently measured competition outcome. Within the retained set, fitted simulations predict that starvation selects for high ploidy and sustained abundance selects for low ploidy. These are model-supported selection predictions, not empirical competition results, and their strength varies across cellular, model, and initial-condition contexts; within-set agreement does not establish robustness across the full candidate model family.

## Results Text

To determine how ploidy changes the response to glucose limitation, we profiled matched lower- and higher-ploidy states from five cell-line backgrounds across a range of starting-glucose concentrations. Time-resolved microscopy and extracellular-glucose measurements provided paired live-cell, dead-cell, and glucose trajectories, while object segmentation and classification converted the images into cell counts (Figure 1a; Figure S1a). Manual alive-point counts closely tracked manual alive-object labels across the annotation set (Pearson r = 0.997), and target-scoped classifier performance supported live-cell calling (ROC AUC = 0.809; balanced accuracy = 0.750; Figure S2a,b). Measurement coverage was nearly complete across cell line, ploidy, and glucose conditions; censoring was confined to six SUM-159-fuse condition-by-glucose combinations (Figure S2c,d). Glucose calibration slopes were positive in every calibration set, although residual error varied and was greatest for SUM-159-fuse (Figure S2e-g). Together, these checks established an adequate measurement framework for comparing starvation responses while identifying SUM-159-fuse glucose measurements as the principal calibration-sensitive subset.

We next asked whether the matched ploidy states exhibited the expected relationship between ploidy and cell size. Across 44 matched condition-level comparisons, elevated-to-baseline cell-area and nuclear-area ratios were strongly positively associated (Pearson r = 0.891), indicating that ploidy-associated increases in cell size were generally accompanied by larger nuclei (Figure 1b,c). Line-level summaries reinforced this relationship: elevated-ploidy MCF10A, SNU668, and SUM-159-chem cells had larger cell and nuclear areas, whereas the smaller ploidy contrast in MDA-MB-231 produced little cell-area separation (Figure 1d). SUM-159-fuse was a clear exception. Despite an approximately twofold ploidy contrast, its elevated-ploidy state had lower cell area and little supported nuclear-area increase (Figure 1b-d). Thus, cell size generally increased with ploidy, but SUM-159-fuse uncoupled higher ploidy from a larger-cell phenotype.

The trajectories then revealed how these matched states responded as glucose became limiting. In the four non-SUM-159-fuse lines displayed in the main trajectory panels, live-cell counts generally accumulated when starting glucose was abundant, peaked and declined at intermediate glucose, and declined under glucose-free conditions (Figure 1e). The complete five-line atlas showed the same nutrient-dependent structure, together with increasing dead-cell counts and condition-specific separation between low- and high-ploidy trajectories (Figure S1a). The direction and magnitude of ploidy separation nevertheless varied among cell lines and glucose conditions: high-ploidy endpoints exceeded low-ploidy endpoints in several MCF10A and SUM-159-chem conditions, whereas SNU668 showed the opposite ordering across the displayed main-panel conditions (Figure 1e). Extracellular glucose declined over time in every displayed main-panel trajectory. Cultures starting at 1 mM approached depletion, whereas those starting at 25 mM retained substantial glucose over the measurement window; high-minus-low endpoint differences were mixed rather than uniformly directional (Figure 1f). These raw curves therefore establish culture-level glucose depletion but do not by themselves identify a uniform ploidy effect on per-cell glucose-consumption rate. Taken together, the measurements show that ploidy modulates growth and glucose-starvation responses, while the direction and magnitude of the raw phenotype depend on cellular background and nutrient context.

## Results Text

The trajectories established that ploidy altered glucose-starvation responses, but they did not distinguish whether this reflected growth, persistence, glucose use per unit live-cell time, or cell production. We therefore extracted complementary features from the observed time courses. A representative trajectory illustrates the smoothed growth derivative, live-cell area under the curve (AUC) over the glucose-measurement window, and net total-cell yield (Figure 2B). The corresponding MDA-MB-231 profiles show how growth, glucose drawdown, and cell yield varied across starting glucose concentrations in low- and high-ploidy cultures (Figure 2C).

We first asked whether high ploidy preserved viable-cell time during the most severe glucose restriction. At 0 mM glucose, live-cell AUC was higher in high-ploidy cultures in all four cell lines in the standardized summary (Figure 2A). The raw all-line comparison showed the same ordering in all five cell lines, including SUM-159-fuse (Figure S3). Thus, despite heterogeneity in other trajectory features, these data show that high-ploidy cultures were more robust to starvation in the direct sense that they maintained more live-cell hours in the absence of supplied glucose.

We next quantified glucose consumption relative to the live-cell time available to consume it. For each cell line and ploidy state, we fitted an intercept-bearing regression of glucose drawdown against live-cell AUC at starting glucose concentrations of 1 mM or less; the slope measures glucose consumed per live-cell hour. The fitted coefficient was higher in high ploidy in each of the four cell lines summarized in Figure 2A. Expressed per 1,000 live-cell hours, the low- versus high-ploidy coefficients were 0.00249 versus 0.00462 for MCF10A, 0.01551 versus 0.01681 for MDA-MB-231, 0.01325 versus 0.01921 for SNU668, and 0.01866 versus 0.02659 for SUM-159-chem (Figures 2A and S4). These data show that high ploidy increased the fitted glucose-consumption rate per live-cell hour across the four-line primary comparison. This coefficient is a culture-level relationship between glucose drawdown and live-cell AUC, rather than a direct measurement of single-cell uptake or metabolic flux. Consistent with broader context dependence, SUM-159-fuse showed the opposite ordering: 0.01672 in low ploidy versus 0.01267 in high ploidy per 1,000 live-cell hours (Figures S3 and S4).

The remaining features defined the limits of these shared effects. At 1 mM glucose, net total-cell yield was lower in high ploidy in all four Figure 2A comparisons, although the MDA-MB-231 values were nearly identical; SUM-159-fuse again reversed this ordering (Figure S3). Because this feature is an unnormalized change in total-cell number, it supports reduced cell output at fixed low glucose but does not by itself establish a per-cell or volumetric yield difference. Growth features were also heterogeneous: low-glucose and high-glucose growth effects crossed zero among cell lines, and the live-AUC intercept did not shift uniformly (Figures 2A and S3). Across the full glucose series, growth and yield profiles likewise varied by cell line and ploidy, and several yield trajectories peaked before the highest glucose condition (Figure S4). Taken together, the direct features identify a shared high-ploidy phenotype of greater live-cell persistence without glucose and, in four cell lines, greater glucose consumption per live-cell hour, while growth and total-cell output remain strongly context dependent.

## Results Text

The direct trajectory features established that ploidy alters glucose-starvation responses, but they did not identify the dynamic processes that generate those differences. We therefore compared a family of ordinary differential equation models that linked measured glucose to growth and allowed additional structure through a hidden second resource, allocation between energy and biomass production, maintenance, and optional waste effects. Crossing four resource configurations with three waste modes produced 12 candidate structures spanning measured-glucose-only, two-resource, and waste-enabled models (Figure 3A).

Comparing the optimized target against operational model complexity supported a set of alternatives rather than a single resolved biochemical mechanism. Across the five Pareto-front candidates, the optimized-target score T, defined as -2 times the best optimized Stan target, decreased as the operational mechanistic complexity count k increased, from the 1R model at k = 24 to the flexible two-resource model with multiplicative waste at k = 66 (Figure 3B). Because the optimized target included priors and Jacobian adjustment, T is not a likelihood deviance, and k is not a full parameter count. The flexible two-resource waste model also had the lowest complexity-penalized optimized-target score, T + 2k, among the 12 assessed structures, whereas the 1R model had a delta complexity-penalized optimized-target score of 4,346.988 (Figure S5B). This score is not conventional AIC. The score advantage was not explained simply by optimization stability: the fraction of starts ending within one log-posterior unit of the best solution varied substantially among models and did not track Pareto membership (Figure S5A). The added operational complexity was nevertheless explicit and structured, arising from additional resource-kinetic, allocation, and waste-kinetic effects at the line and ploidy levels (Figure S5C). We therefore retained a mechanistically diverse five-model Pareto set for subsequent inference rather than selecting one latent mechanism as uniquely established. This downstream set was defined by an arbitrary optimized-target filter, so comparisons among its members do not establish robustness across the full 12-structure family.

We next asked whether the advantage of richer structures was confined to a small subset of conditions. Unlike the optimized-target quantity in Figure 3B, Figure 3C reports condition-specific observation-likelihood loss per well relative to the best of the five retained models. This loss varied jointly with model, cell line, ploidy state, and starting glucose in the analysis excluding SUM-159-fuse (Figure 3C). The 1R model incurred substantial losses in several conditions, whereas the flexible two-resource and waste-enabled candidates more often approached the best local fit, although each retained isolated regions of higher loss. Thus, hidden-resource and waste structure improved the simultaneous description of heterogeneous trajectories, but no candidate was uniformly best across every condition.

The fitted trajectory atlases and corrected latent-state summaries show where these models differ while also defining the boundary of mechanistic interpretation. Across all five lines, the models produced distinct time courses in the blocks labeled N, R1, W1, and R2, with separations depending on ploidy and starting glucose (Figure S6A-E). Within the model, R1 denotes measured glucose, whereas R2 and W1 are model-implied states rather than directly measured environmental variables. Accordingly, the high-minus-low net-growth surfaces and phase-space displays visualize line-, model-, and glucose-dependent latent-state relationships, but they do not identify R2 as a particular nutrient or W1 as a particular waste product (Figure S8A; Figure S9A). The fraction-positive summaries were likewise heterogeneous across lines, with SUM-159-fuse displaying a visibly distinct surface from the other lines (Figure S8B). These latent-state patterns provide concrete hypotheses for why richer models fit better, not direct measurements of the proposed hidden processes.

We first tested whether fitted ploidy effects generalized to an opposite-ploidy state withheld from a target line in arbitrary transfer contexts. The analysis compared held-out log likelihood under a fitted ploidy effect with a null model lacking that effect (Figure S7A). Transfer was not uniformly successful: 77 of 96 line-model-direction scores were negative, including 39 of 48 high-to-low and 38 of 48 low-to-high comparisons, although outcomes varied strongly among lines and models (Figure S7B). The highlighted 2R(f) cases illustrate this heterogeneity. MCF10A low-to-high transfer improved on the null by approximately 373 log-likelihood units, whereas SNU668 high-to-low transfer was worse by approximately 405 units (Figure S7B-D). Thus, a fitted ploidy effect can improve prediction in particular contexts but is not a reliable universal transfer rule.

We then asked the narrower mechanistic question of whether the models could learn an environment that favored a requested ploidy state and retain that sign for the target line. For each reciprocal-state split, one of 10 cell-line/ploidy states was excluded; four complete line pairs defined the selection objective, and the observed state of the target line contributed to the viability constraint. The resulting environment was frozen and assessed with the target-inclusive 10-of-10 fitted reference (Figure S14A). Across the fixed, arbitrarily filtered set of five model families, five target lines, and two holdout directions, model-native searches recovered the requested high-ploidy sign in 35 of 50 splits under both the exact and robust criteria, compared with 8 of 50 for the common-R1 search (Figure S14B). The advantage was not symmetric across objectives: for environments intended to favor low ploidy, model-native searches succeeded in 17 of 50 splits under the exact criterion and 12 of 50 under the robust criterion, only modestly above the corresponding common-R1 results of 15 of 50 and 10 of 50.

This transfer depended on the model and target rather than defining a general rule. For the high-ploidy objective, the model-native improvement was concentrated in the three two-resource families, which attained the requested sign in 8-10 of 10 splits, whereas the two one-resource families were unchanged because both search modes used the same R1-only grid (Figure S14C). All five target lines improved under model-native search, attaining the requested sign in 5-8 of 10 splits. In contrast, the low-ploidy totals changed only for one model family and one target line. These fractions are descriptive summaries of fitted model-line-direction tasks, and the assessment uses a target-inclusive fitted reference rather than an independently measured competition outcome. The corrected reciprocal-state benchmark therefore identifies transferable ploidy-by-environment relationships in some fitted model families within the retained set while retaining strong objective-, search-mode-, model-family-, and target-line dependence.

Taken together, the model comparison supports hidden-resource and waste structure as a better description of glucose-starvation dynamics than measured glucose alone. The latent variables remain mechanistic hypotheses, and the two transfer analyses jointly show that fitted ploidy effects can generalize in selected model-native environments but are not universally transportable across contexts.

## Results Text

We next asked whether the fitted effect of ploidy was large and mechanistically consistent relative to variation among cell lines and model structures. To compare these sources of variation on a common scale, we represented posterior-median values of four apparent parameters—yield (`y1`), uptake (`v1`), glucose response (`K1`), and maintenance/death (`m`)—after global standardization across five models admitted by an arbitrary optimized-target filter, four cell lines, and two ploidy states. In the resulting parameter space, low-to-high-ploidy displacements were aligned within each model but occupied different backgrounds across lines and model structures (Figure 4a,b). Exact line-profile distances ranged from 0.80 to 1.57 standardized RMS units, with MCF10A and SNU668 the most separated pair (Figure 4c). By comparison, exact within-model ploidy distances ranged from 0.20 to 0.79 and remained at or below the smallest line-profile distance; the displacement was especially small for MDA-MB-231, consistent with its smaller ploidy contrast (Figure 4d). Model-centroid distances spanned a much broader relative range, from 0.19 for the closely related 1R pair to 1.32 between 2R(a) and 2R(f),W(m) (Figure 4e). Thus, cell-line background was the largest consistent source of fitted variation, ploidy produced a smaller within-model shift, and model structure altered the fitted background by amounts ranging from minor to the same order as cell-line identity.

The parameter decomposition identified two directions that were stable across this hierarchy. All 20 displayed `v1` contributions were positive, whereas all 20 `m` contributions were negative across the five models and four lines (Figure 4f). These results show that the fitted high-ploidy state consistently had higher apparent glucose uptake and lower maintenance/death pressure in the primary four-line analysis. In contrast, the apparent-yield contribution depended on model structure: `y1` was negative in the 1R, 1R,W(m), and 2R(a) models but slightly positive in both flexible 2R models. `K1` was positive throughout the primary analysis, but its biological interpretation was less direct and its magnitude changed with model and line-set scope (Figure 4f; Figure S10b). Model structure therefore changed the mechanistic allocation of the ploidy effect, particularly among yield, glucose response, and maintenance/death, without changing the recurrent higher-`v1` and lower-`m` directions.

Posterior uncertainty conditional on each model was small relative to the displayed ploidy displacement: the 80% displacement-ellipse radius was smaller than its corresponding arrow in all 20 model-by-line comparisons, with a median radius-to-arrow ratio of approximately 0.10 (Figure 4a). This comparison does not rank posterior uncertainty against model-choice uncertainty. Rather, the ellipses quantify uncertainty conditional on a model and dataset, whereas the separation among model centroids and the changing `y1` decomposition show structural sensitivity across models (Figure 4a,e,f). The selected no-SUM and all-line fits showed no displayed diagnostic flags; the leave-one-line-out inventory contained one minor tail-ESS exception (Figure S10c). These checks support the stability of the fitted summaries while preserving model structure as an important boundary on mechanistic interpretation.

Because `y1` and `v1` are expressed per cell, we next placed these effects in cell-size context. Adding adjusted-area or latent-volume-proxy ratios shifted several yield summaries toward positive values, but `y1` remained mixed across models under every displayed definition. By contrast, all 60 `v1` model intervals remained positive across per-cell, area-filled, and volume-proxy-filled views (Figure S10a). Including SUM-159-fuse generally attenuated the fitted effects: the all-line summaries retained positive `v1` and negative `m` point estimates, whereas `K1` moved closer to zero and remained the most scope-sensitive parameter (Figure S10b). The leave-one-line-out display was directionally concordant, with positive `v1` and negative `m` summaries across exclusions (Figure S13a). This result therefore provides sensitivity context rather than an independent quantitative estimate. Taken together, the posterior evidence identifies higher apparent uptake and lower maintenance/death as the most consistent fitted high-ploidy changes within the retained five-model set, superimposed on larger line-specific backgrounds and substantial within-set model dependence in yield and response-scale interpretation.

## Results Text

We next asked whether the fitted growth models predicted an ecological consequence when low- and high-ploidy cells competed for the same simulated resource. We simulated 50:50 mixtures over six days under three-stage glucose schedules and scored selection by the final log ratio of live high- to low-ploidy cells. Positive endpoints therefore favor high ploidy, whereas negative endpoints favor low ploidy. The reported ranking effectively used this unconstrained directional score because schedules that failed the expansion criterion fell back to the raw score; whether viability was intended to exclude such schedules remains unresolved. Across 448 schedules and five fitted model categories admitted by an arbitrary optimized-target filter, higher starting glucose generally shifted the no-SUM endpoints toward low-ploidy enrichment, while lower-resource or carry schedules contained regions favoring high ploidy (Figure S11A).

The retrospectively selected primary-model examples within this retained set illustrate how these reciprocal outcomes emerged. The high-ploidy-favoring schedule began at 5 mM glucose and carried the residual resource without refresh at days 2 and 4, producing progressive depletion. By day 6, the simulated high-ploidy fraction had risen from 50% to 96.0% in MCF10A, 62.2% in MDA-MB-231, and 54.8% in SUM-159-chem, whereas SNU668 remained at 45.0%. In contrast, the low-ploidy-favoring schedule began at 25 mM and refreshed cultures to 25 mM at days 2 and 4. Residual glucose remained abundant, and the high-ploidy fraction fell in all four displayed lines, reaching 13.8% in MCF10A, 47.5% in MDA-MB-231, 8.9% in SNU668, and 9.8% in SUM-159-chem (Figure 5A,B). Thus, within the fitted primary-model simulations, starvation selected for high ploidy at the aggregate level, whereas sustained abundance selected for low ploidy, with cell-line heterogeneity in the magnitude and even the direction of the starvation response.

The aggregate model comparison tested whether this prediction survived changes in model structure and inclusion of the corrected SUM-159-fuse data. For the starvation objective, mean day-6 log(high/low) was positive in all five model categories in both the four-line no-SUM and corrected five-line SUM-included scopes; the selected endpoints favored high ploidy in 75–100% of no-SUM line contexts and in all SUM-included line contexts (Figure 5C). For the abundance objective, all five no-SUM model means were negative. In the corrected SUM-included scope, the primary model, the retained 2R(a) alternative, and the 1R comparator retained negative mean endpoints, whereas two other retained models moved close to zero and one of these crossed slightly positive. The resource-dependent reversal is therefore strongest in the primary model and consistent across the no-SUM retained set, while the low-ploidy prediction becomes model dependent when SUM-159-fuse is included.

Initial conditions further changed the predicted selection response. At a 50:50 mixture, seven of eight no-SUM density-response series increased in final high-ploidy fraction as initial total-cell number rose; the remaining series was effectively unchanged (Figure 5D). The broader grid showed that changes in final high-ploidy fraction also depended strongly on the seeded high-ploidy fraction, and many model-context combinations changed objective-success direction relative to the baseline initial condition (Figure S11B). These results identify resource depletion, starting density, and initial composition as interacting determinants of simulated ploidy selection rather than fixed properties of a cell line or model.

Leave-one-line sensitivity analyses separated stable from sensitive parts of this prediction within the retained model set. In the no-SUM scope, omitting any one of the four cell lines preserved the objective-favoring direction for both starvation and abundance in all five model categories. With the corrected SUM-included simulations, every starvation-objective score remained positive after omission of any one of the five lines. The abundance objective remained directionally stable for the primary, 2R(a), and 1R comparator models, but the two near-zero retained models contained omissions that preserved or reversed the intended direction (Figure S12C). Including SUM-159-fuse therefore did not erase the primary resource-dependent prediction, but it exposed a specific limit to its within-set stability under abundant-resource schedules.

These outcomes are predictions from mixed-population simulations parameterized by fitted monoculture models, not observations from empirical competition assays. They are conditional on the arbitrary five-model filter and on the implemented raw-score fallback rather than a viability-constrained ranking. Taken together, the fitted simulations predict that resource starvation selects for high ploidy and sustained resource abundance selects for low ploidy, while also defining the model and initial-condition regimes in which those predictions weaken.

## Manuscript Text

Ploidy modulates response to glucose starvation. Across matched lower- and higher-ploidy states, this modulation appeared in live-cell persistence, culture-level glucose drawdown, cell production, morphology, fitted dynamic parameters, and simulated ecological outcomes. The common signal was not a uniform response across all cell lines or nutrient conditions. Instead, high ploidy produced a context-dependent cost-benefit structure in which greater starvation persistence and glucose use were balanced against reduced cell-number yield, with cellular background and model structure determining the magnitude and, for some traits, the direction of the effect.

High-ploidy cells are more robust to starvation in the direct sense supported by the experiments: at 0 mM supplied glucose, high-ploidy cultures accumulated more live-cell hours in all five cell-line or derivation groups. This result defines robustness as persistence under glucose-free conditions, not as universal resistance to other stresses. High ploidy also increases glucose consumption rate within the primary four-cell-line comparison. Here, the rate is the slope from an intercept-bearing regression of glucose-concentration drawdown on live-cell area under the curve, and therefore represents culture-level glucose drawdown per live-cell hour. It is not live-cell hours per unit glucose, an absolute glucose amount per cell-hour, direct single-cell uptake, or metabolic flux. The direction was higher in high ploidy in each of the four primary cell lines but reversed in SUM-159-fuse, establishing an important boundary on its generality.

High ploidy decreases yield per cell in the model-supported cost-benefit interpretation, meaning lower apparent cell production per glucose consumed. The direct features are consistent with this interpretation because high-ploidy cultures produced fewer net total cells at fixed low glucose in the four primary comparisons, but that measurement is not itself normalized per cell, glucose consumed, or biomass. Moreover, fitted apparent yield, `y1`, changed direction among model structures and therefore should not be presented as a uniformly negative posterior effect. Cell size is proportional to ploidy in the dominant cross-line pattern: higher-ploidy states generally had larger cell and nuclear areas. SUM-159-fuse again formed an informative exception, uncoupling its approximately twofold ploidy difference from a larger-cell phenotype, while the small ploidy contrast in MDA-MB-231 produced little area separation. Size therefore provides essential context for per-cell quantities without establishing that size mediates the ploidy effect.

These observations motivate the explicit working hypothesis that high ploidy increases yield (volumetric). Larger high-ploidy cells combined with lower cell-number yield could still produce more biomass or cellular volume per unit resource. Current area-filled and latent-volume-proxy transformations shift some yield estimates in that direction, but the results remain mixed across models and are not direct volumetric-yield measurements. Volumetric yield should therefore remain a testable working hypothesis, distinct from the supported model-based conclusion that high ploidy decreases yield per cell.

Within an operational five-model set admitted by an arbitrary optimized-target filter, the fitted parameter geometry further defines what is shared and what remains contingent. Cell-line background was the largest consistent source of fitted variation, whereas the within-model ploidy shift was smaller; changes among retained model structures ranged from minor to approximately cell-line-sized. Superimposed on that hierarchy, high ploidy recurrently shifted apparent `v1` upward and maintenance/death parameter `m` downward across all five retained models and four primary cell lines. These directions support higher model-inferred apparent uptake and lower starvation-associated maintenance or death pressure, but neither parameter is a direct biochemical or survival measurement. By contrast, `y1` remained model-dependent, and the glucose-response parameter `K1` was less directly interpretable and particularly sensitive to whether SUM-159-fuse was included. The displayed ellipses quantify posterior uncertainty conditional on a chosen model and dataset, whereas differences among model centroids and parameter decompositions show sensitivity within the retained model set. The figure therefore distinguishes, but does not quantitatively rank, posterior uncertainty and model-choice sensitivity, and within-set agreement does not establish robustness across the full candidate family.

Richer models incorporating a second resource or waste effect described the heterogeneous trajectories better than measured-glucose-only models, but their latent states remain hypotheses. The second-resource and waste variables were not directly measured and cannot yet be assigned to particular nutrients, metabolites, or intracellular processes. Likewise, reciprocal held-out-ploidy likelihood transfer in arbitrary contexts was mixed and was worse than the matched null in most cell-line, model, and direction combinations. That result remains the principal boundary on transportability: a fitted ploidy effect can improve an unobserved-state likelihood in selected cases, but it is not a reliable universal transfer rule.

A narrower fitted-model benchmark qualifies rather than reverses this limitation. After environments were selected to favor a requested ploidy direction, some model-native environments within the retained operational set preserved that direction in the target-inclusive fitted reference. For the high-ploidy objective, model-native searches met both the exact and robust criteria in 35 of 50 fitted model-line-direction tasks, with gains concentrated in the two-resource model families and present to varying degrees across all five target lines. For the low-ploidy objective, improvement over common-R1 search was modest: exact success increased from 15 of 50 to 17 of 50 tasks and robust success from 10 of 50 to 12 of 50. Thus, requested-direction retention depended on the objective, search mode, model family, and target line. These fixed-task fractions are descriptive and have no inferential interpretation, and the reference was a target-inclusive 10-of-10 fit rather than an independently measured competition outcome. The benchmark therefore does not provide prospective empirical validation, establish broad transportability across arbitrary environments, or establish robustness beyond the arbitrarily filtered model set.

The main ecological payoff is a pair of model-supported predictions within the retained operational set: starvation selects for high ploidy, whereas abundance selects for low ploidy. These claims arise from mixed-state simulations parameterized by monoculture fits, not from empirical mixed-culture competition assays. Under resource-depleting schedules, the aggregate fitted response favored high ploidy across the assessed retained model categories and remained positive in the five-line analysis including SUM-159-fuse. Under sustained glucose abundance, the primary model and the complete four-cell-line retained model set favored low ploidy, but the prediction became model-dependent after SUM-159-fuse was included, with two other retained models approaching zero and one crossing slightly toward high ploidy. Both predictions also depended on starting density, initial high-ploidy fraction, refresh schedule, and cell-line background. The simulations used best-fit parameters without propagating posterior uncertainty and ranked schedules by unconstrained directional enrichment rather than a viability constraint. They consequently identify conditions under which selection is expected, rather than demonstrating that those outcomes occur in competition; because the five models were admitted by an arbitrary optimized-target filter, agreement within this set does not establish robustness across the full candidate family.

Several unresolved study limitations constrain reproducibility and interpretation. Complete culture conditions, design records, and the basis of biological replication remain unavailable. The exact production segmentation and classifier state cannot be fully linked to the analyzed counts. The executed glucose-assay protocol and empirical basis for the lower measurement convention are not established. Representative-model selection retains a retrospective visual criterion and lacks a deterministic rule for an exact tie. The biological justification for ranking simulated schedules by unconstrained directional enrichment remains to be confirmed. Finally, a complete archival release still requires stable code and data deposition, licenses and citation metadata, frozen software environments and external model weights, and durable access conditions. These gaps do not erase the observed and fitted patterns, but they limit independent reproduction and should preclude stronger procedural claims.

The most decisive next experiments follow directly from these boundaries. Isotope tracing or calibrated extracellular-flux measurements paired with live-cell counts and cell-volume measurements could test whether the culture-level drawdown coefficient reflects greater per-cell or per-volume glucose uptake. Joint measurements of cell number, dry mass, protein, and biovolume per glucose consumed could distinguish reduced cell-number yield from the volumetric-yield hypothesis. Barcoded or otherwise distinguishable low- and high-ploidy cells should be competed directly under controlled depletion and refresh schedules while varying starting density and mixture composition. Metabolomic profiling, conditioned-medium exchange, and targeted nutrient supplementation could test candidate identities for the latent resource and waste states. Finally, prospective tests across additional matched backgrounds and independently generated ploidy states could separate effects of ploidy from cell size, induction history, and other persistent cellular states. Together, such experiments would determine whether the context-dependent tradeoff identified here generalizes into a causal principle of ploidy-dependent resource ecology.

### Study Design And Analysis Scope

We analyzed matched lower- and higher-ploidy variants across five cell-line or derivation groups from four underlying line backgrounds: MCF10A, MDA-MB-231, SNU668, SUM-159-chem, and SUM-159-fuse. The panel included one gastric cancer background, SNU668; two breast cancer backgrounds, MDA-MB-231 and SUM-159; and one non-transformed breast epithelial background, MCF10A. The matched higher-ploidy variants were generated by distinct routes: chemically induced cytokinesis failure for MCF10A, phosphate and glutamine limitation for MDA-MB-231, long-term r-selection conditions for SNU668, and either chemical polyploidization or cell fusion for the two SUM-159 derivations. The paired lines maintained differential chromosome number through more than 10 passages; the MCF10A isogenic lines were obtained from the laboratory of Mark E. Burkard.

Each group contributed microscopy-derived cell counts, extracellular-glucose measurements, starting-glucose condition, experiment, well, ploidy state, and elapsed time. The primary analysis excluded SUM-159-fuse and retained MCF10A, MDA-MB-231, SNU668, and SUM-159-chem. The five-group data set, including SUM-159-fuse, was used for sensitivity analyses of direct features, posterior parameter effects, and fitted selection simulations.

Ploidy state was represented both by line-specific state labels and by a scalar ploidy metric. The paired states were 2N and 4N for MCF10A, SUM-159-chem, and SUM-159-fuse; 3N and parental for MDA-MB-231; and low and high for SNU668. Absolute ploidy values were mapped to 2.0 and 4.0 for MCF10A, SUM-159-chem, and SUM-159-fuse; 3.5 and 4.4 for MDA-MB-231; and 2.6 and 5.2 for SNU668. Within each line, the scalar metric was the log2 ratio of a state's mapped ploidy to the lowest mapped ploidy for that line, rounded to three decimals. The lower state was therefore coded 0 for every line, whereas the higher state was coded 1.00 for MCF10A, SNU668, SUM-159-chem, and SUM-159-fuse and 0.33 for MDA-MB-231.

Starting glucose was represented by seven nominal concentrations: 0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 5, and 25 mM. Longitudinal imaging was generally collected approximately every 8 h through 120 h. One SUM-159-fuse experiment was imaged from 8 through 144 h, with 8 h treated as the analysis start and seeding as time zero; a second spanned 0--120 h with 15 recorded time points and one 16-h interval. MCF10A cultures were seeded one day before treatment in unspecified normal medium, washed on day 0, and placed directly into glucose-defined medium without a 6-h starvation step. Other lines were held for 6 h in unspecified starvation medium before 2x glucose medium was added to reach the final concentration.

MCF10A experiments used 48-well plates. The analyzed MCF10A experiments assigned four physical wells to each ploidy-by-glucose condition and acquired nine imaging fields per well; MCF10A seeding density was not documented. MDA-MB-231, SNU668, and SUM-159 experiments used 96-well plates. MDA-MB-231, SNU668, and SUM-159-chem were seeded at 3,500 cells per well, with four physical wells and four imaging fields per ploidy-by-glucose condition. SUM-159-fuse was also seeded at 3,500 cells per well; its two experiments used six wells across five glucose conditions or four wells across five glucose conditions, with four imaging fields per well. The physical well was treated as the within-run replicate and longitudinal analysis unit. For each physical well and time, available object calls from all represented imaging fields were summed: alive and dead counts were the respective numbers of alive- and dead-labeled objects, whereas junk-labeled objects and objects with missing class labels contributed to neither count. Counts were not divided by the number or area of fields, corrected for possible field overlap, or deduplicated across fields. Imaging fields therefore served as technical sampling areas within wells rather than independent observations. Field identity and the number of contributing fields were not retained, and fields with no segmented objects or processing failures did not generate explicit zero-field records. Consequently, whether every scheduled field contributed at every time and whether missing fields changed the summed count scale could not be established. Experiment labels denoted separate acquisition runs, but independent biological-experiment, passage, and culture-batch replication could not be established; randomization, blinding, blocking, and exclusion or replacement rules were also undocumented. Consequently, count trajectories, direct features, and fitted-model results are conditional on the observed wells and acquisition runs and do not provide replication-based inference to a defined population of independent biological experiments.

Alive-channel labels differed among lines and included red-nuclear, green-nuclear, orange, or paired red/green signals, whereas dead objects were recorded in near-infrared channels. These labels do not establish the reporter constructs, fluorescent proteins, stains, concentrations, or labeling procedures.

**Experimental-design and biological-replication limitation:** Basal medium formulations and supplements, incubation conditions, MCF10A seeding density, plate brand or coating and working volume, reporter or stain identities and preparation, and definitions of independent biological experiments, culture batches, randomization, blinding, blocking, and exclusions were not established. The biological design is therefore not fully reproducible, and the reported comparisons should be interpreted as conditional on the observed within-run wells.

The primary four-group scope was used for direct-feature summaries, primary mechanistic-model comparisons, posterior parameter geometry, transfer/null stress tests, and primary mixed-state selection simulations. Five-group analyses using the validated SUM-159-fuse mapping were used as sensitivity or context analyses. Directional transfer tasks held out one ploidy state within a target line: low-to-high tasks trained on the lower-ploidy target state and predicted the higher-ploidy state, whereas high-to-low tasks reversed those roles. Other lines remained in the training set. Matched null fits used the same held-out wells but removed ploidy information from the fitted design.

### Microscopy Acquisition, Segmentation, And Object Classification

Raw microscopy inputs were IncuCyte-exported TIFF image groups containing phase, alive-channel, and dead-channel images. Filename-derived metadata assigned cell line, experiment, plate position, ploidy state, starting glucose, and elapsed time. Channel tags were normalized to a common phase/dead/alive schema, with two alive-channel classes retained for MDA-MB-231. The analysis records identify the IncuCyte software family and image structure but do not establish the instrument model, objective, magnification, illumination, exposure, autofocus settings, or pixel size.

Objects were segmented with Cellpose. Phase contrast and the sum of all non-phase fluorescence channels were each clipped at their first and 99th intensity percentiles, independently rescaled to the range 0--1, and stacked as a two-channel segmentation image. Automatic diameter selection was used. The production script did not specify a Cellpose model name, channel vector, flow threshold, or cell-probability threshold, and the historical package and weight versions were not captured. All nonzero mask objects were passed to classification unless no objects were detected; no border-touching or size filter was applied in the production segmentation stream.

Object classification used pseudo-RGB composites ordered as dead, alive, and phase, with each channel independently clipped at its first and 99th percentiles and rescaled to 0--1. For each object, a masked tight crop used the exact bounding box and an unmasked context crop was centered on that box and extended approximately three bounding-box heights and widths, subject to image boundaries. Both crops were bilinearly resized to 224 x 224 pixels with antialiasing and normalized with ImageNet means (0.485, 0.456, 0.406) and standard deviations (0.229, 0.224, 0.225). A ResNet18 requested the default pretrained weights and produced a 512-value embedding from each crop; the documented implementation resolved this request to ImageNet-1K V1, but the exact production checkpoint was not captured. Concatenation gave 1,024 classifier inputs.

A fixed three-class Random Forest assigned objects to alive, dead, or junk. It used 100 trees, Gini splitting, square-root feature sampling, bootstrap samples, unlimited tree depth, minimum split and leaf sizes of 2 and 1, no pruning, no out-of-bag scoring, no class weights, and random seed 42. The adjacent annotation record contained 57 nonzero manual labels from 23 of 100 intended training images: 28 alive, 21 dead, and 8 junk. The implemented training procedure fit once to all labeled objects, without a training/tuning split, cross-validation, hyperparameter search, resampling, class-balance correction, or probability-threshold tuning. Because the serialized classifier does not retain its training rows or command, these documented labels cannot be shown to be the exact training set used to fit it, and linkage between the serialized classifier and every production count was not established. Object-level outputs included class calls and probabilities. Alive and dead counts were the numbers receiving the corresponding hard class call; junk objects were excluded.

Classifier validation used a 90-frame annotation stack sampled independently across cell line, ploidy, glucose bin, and within-stratum time tertile. The sampled frame identifiers did not overlap the intended 100-image training list, although incomplete classifier-training records prevent an absolute claim that all validation frames were unseen throughout development. Validation used a 400-pixel centered crop and a 200-pixel central annotation target. Manual ImageJ alive-cell points, Cellpose masks, and classifier calls were treated as distinct measurement layers. Manual points were matched to segmented objects with a 3-pixel snap radius after border-touching validation objects were excluded. Binary validation mapped alive to alive and pooled dead and junk as not alive; it did not assess three-class annotation accuracy. Object-level summaries included accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC area under the curve, average precision, log loss, and confusion-matrix counts. Frame-level validation compared manual alive-object counts with hard-call alive counts and summed alive probabilities using bias, mean absolute error, root-mean-square error, and Pearson and Spearman correlations. No probability threshold or pass/fail cutoff was imposed for production calling.

**Production segmentation and classification limitation:** The exact production Cellpose package, model or weights, channel and threshold settings, software environment, classifier-training rows and executed training command, serialized classifier identity, and linkage from that classifier to the production counts were not established. The image-analysis pipeline is therefore not fully reproducible.

### Glucose Assay Processing And Calibration

Extracellular glucose was measured with assays identified in the records as the Promega Glucose-Glo family. The executed sample collection, storage, reagent preparation, assay volumes, mixing, and incubation procedures were not recorded. Microplate-reader exports for the 2022 and 2025 assays used a Thermo Scientific Varioskan LUX with a 384-well adapter, normal luminescence optics, automatic dynamic range, no smaller aperture, and a 1,000-ms measurement time. Settle delay was off in the 2022 run and on in the 2025 runs. Luminescence was reported in relative light units. The instrument and settings for an older SUM-159-fuse assay were not recorded.

Assay workbooks were manually curated into processed luminescence and calibration-standard tables. The processed observation tables contained separate assay-well luminescence readings, cell line, starting-glucose condition, assay day, dilution factor, and ploidy fields. Most tables contained three luminescence readings per sample condition; the older SUM-159-fuse table contained as many as four. Their relationship to culture wells or split aliquots was not recorded, so these readings were not interpreted as independent biological replicates. Analysis assembly used the replicate luminescence values rather than workbook averages or workbook-derived glucose columns. In the mechanistic likelihood, retained luminescence readings contributed separate observation terms conditional on the modeled trajectory and fixed calibration; correlation among readings from the same sample, aliquot, or culture well was not modeled. The workbook-to-processed-table conversion was manual and lacked an executable row-level map.

Replicate luminescence columns were converted to long format. Empty strings, missing values, and text `NA` were treated as missing and removed. Older records denoted low signals as `LO` and summary values as less than 20; no measured value below 20 was retained. Such observations were assigned the analysis bound 20.0 and flagged as left censored. In the mechanistic likelihood they contributed a lognormal cumulative probability at 20 rather than a point density. The value 20 is an analysis censoring convention, not an observed luminescence value or a validated detector limit; the experimental basis for it was not recoverable. If the recorded dilution factor was `X`, the dimensionless model multiplier was `D = 1` for `X = 1` and `D = 1000/X` otherwise. Thus factors 10, 100, and 500 became multipliers 100, 10, and 2, respectively, whereas the older factor 1,000 became 1. Assay days were converted to elapsed hours and matched to the count-data structure.

Each calibration batch contributed known glucose standards and luminescence readings. The three later assay workbooks labeled calibration concentrations in mM. One calibration series used three readings at 0, 0.004, 0.02, 0.1, 0.5, 2, 10, and 50 mM, and the others used two readings at those concentrations plus 25 mM. The older assay recorded standards at 18, 90, and 450 mg/dL; these were represented in the processed calibration as 1, 5, and 25 mM using `G_mM = G_mg/dL/18`. Standard preparation, blanks, linearity limits, and plate-coordinate origins were unavailable. Fixed calibration parameters were estimated separately by batch with the positive linear relation

`Lum = aG + b`,

where `G` is glucose concentration in mM, `a` has luminescence units per mM, and `b` has luminescence units. Parameters were estimated by minimizing squared log residuals. Calibration noise was set to the larger of 0.1 and the standard deviation of log residuals when enough residuals were available. Review flags used log-root-mean-square error of at least 0.25 or maximum absolute log residual of at least 0.75. These flags were diagnostic and did not exclude observation rows.

Replicate glucose was reconstructed as `G_hat = max[0, (Lum - b)/(aD)]` in mM. For each well and time, the glucose summary was the median of uncensored reconstructed replicates when any were available and otherwise the median of all reconstructed replicates. The mechanistic observation model used the corresponding forward relation `Lum_hat = a R1 D + b`; consequently the first resource `R1`, its fitted initial state, and nominal starting glucose were all in mM. The second resource `R2` was latent, initialized to 1, and had no established physical concentration unit. Glucose initial, final, minimum, and drawdown values were in mM; glucose AUC was in mM-hours; maximum glucose-drawdown rate was in mM/h; the glucose-floor time used a 0.1-mM threshold; and regression of glucose drawdown on live-cell AUC had a slope in mM per cell-hour.

Calibration parameters were treated as fixed in direct-feature construction and mechanistic fitting. Calibration-estimation uncertainty and dependence among replicate luminescence readings were not propagated into direct features, optimized fits, posterior intervals, fitted predictions, or simulations. Those uncertainty summaries are therefore conditional on the fitted calibration curves and the likelihood's separate-observation treatment.

**Glucose-assay and calibration limitation:** The exact kit or reagents, sample collection and storage procedure, reagent preparation and assay volumes, mixing and incubation procedure, meaning of assay-well replicates, validated lower-limit rule, and laboratory rationale for the legacy dilution encoding were not established. The older mg/dL-to-mM conversion and the executable mM concentration chain were recoverable, but the manual workbook-to-table transcription had no row-level executable map. The value 20 remains an analysis censoring convention rather than a validated detector limit. These gaps prevent full assay reproduction even though the analyzed concentration scale is defined.

### Analysis Data Assembly And Quality Control

Analysis-ready data combined alive/dead count observations, processed glucose observations, calibration rows, fixed calibration parameters, censoring flags, well metadata, starting-glucose values, line and ploidy mappings, and a shared time grid. Count rows were standardized by attaching image and condition metadata and indexing observations by well and time. Glucose rows were matched to the same experimental structure and retained luminescence, the dimensionless model multiplier `D`, calibration batch, and censoring variables. Condition-level quality-control summaries described count and glucose coverage, glucose censoring, and calibration residuals.

For the SUM-159-fuse assay table, the ploidy column was identified case-insensitively, its values were trimmed and converted to lower case, and ambiguous columns or labels other than low and high were rejected. The validated labels were then mapped to the line-specific 2N and 4N numeric states before glucose observations were assigned to wells. This validated mapping was used for five-group analyses; exclusion of SUM-159-fuse defined the separate four-group input.

Area and nuclear-size analyses used object-level segmentation/classification outputs with additional retention gates. Alive-cell area summaries required a finite mask area and a hard alive-object call. Nuclear-size summaries came from a bounded selected-image analysis described below. Elevated-versus-baseline nuclear comparisons required finite medians and at least 10 alive cells with detected nuclei in both ploidy states.

### Morphology And Size Summaries

Cell area was the number of pixels assigned to an object's Cellpose mask; no physical pixel calibration was available. The area measurement stream processed all discovered image groups under its default full-sample setting. Hard-classified alive objects with finite area were pooled by cell line, experiment, physical well, ploidy state, exact starting glucose, and exact acquisition hour; imaging fields were pooled within these groups. Quantiles used the R type-7 definition. The q50 and q90 summaries were the median and 90th percentile of alive-object mask-pixel counts. Area-aware modeling required at least 30 alive objects in a group.

Adjusted morphology effects were estimated with separate ordinary least-squares models for `log(q50)`, `log(q90)`, and `log(q50^1.5)`. The last quantity was a latent volume proxy, not a direct volumetric measurement. Each model included ploidy metric, cell line, their interaction, standardized log live-cell density, standardized `log1p` interpolated glucose, standardized hour and squared standardized hour, and a standardized growth-rate proxy. Within each line, the highest-versus-lowest ploidy contrast held the other covariates at their within-line medians. Symmetric estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard-error limits were computed from the ordinary least-squares covariance matrix on the log scale and then exponentiated or converted to log2 ratios. Repeated well, time, and matched count-replicate rows were treated as ordinary observations; experiment and well were not modeled as random effects or cluster units. The displayed 1.96-standard-error limits were used only as descriptive model-based reference ranges under the row-independence assumption. They are not confidence intervals for ploidy effects across independent wells, images, experiments, or biological replicates. A clustered or repeated-measures reanalysis would be required for that inferential interpretation.

The nuclear-size analysis selected one image from each cell-line-by-ploidy-by-glucose-bin-by-time-bin stratum. Glucose bins were low (0 and 0.1 mM), middle (0.25, 0.5, and 1 mM), and high (5 and 25 mM). Within each cell-line, ploidy, and glucose group, images were ordered by acquisition time and divided into equal-count early, middle, and late tertiles; these were rank bins rather than fixed hour ranges. The image nearest the stratum's median hour was selected, with retained-object count and image identifier used as tie breakers. This design selected 90 images, one per stratum, and therefore did not provide within-stratum image replication.

For this selected-image analysis, Cellpose v4 CPSAM received a two-channel input formed from independently first/99th-percentile-normalized phase and summed alive-plus-dead fluorescence, with automatic diameter, channel setting `[2,1]`, and batch size 8. Cell objects smaller than 50 mask pixels were excluded from measurement summaries. Within each retained cell, the alive and dead fluorescence channels were summed and robustly normalized. Cells with fewer than 10 pixels or with a within-cell 95th-minus-5th percentile intensity range below 0.03 were assigned no nucleus. Otherwise the image was Gaussian smoothed with sigma 1 and thresholded by within-cell Otsu thresholding, using the 75th percentile only if Otsu failed and raising the threshold to at least the within-cell 60th percentile. Candidate nuclei were restricted to the cell mask; components smaller than 10 pixels were removed, a radius-1 disk closing and hole filling were applied, and components smaller than 10 pixels were removed again. Nuclear area was the retained nuclear-mask pixel count.

Alive objects in the nuclear analysis were identified by `live_bg_z - dead_bg_z >= 0`. A fallback based on the difference between mean alive- and dead-channel intensity was defined only for cases in which the preferred object-feature table or its table-reading dependency was unavailable; it was not used for the reported summaries. Condition summaries pooled objects by cell line, ploidy, glucose bin, time bin, and exact starting glucose and reported medians of cell mask-pixel count, nuclear mask-pixel count, and their ratio. Elevated-versus-baseline summaries took the median log2 ratio across available glucose/time condition rows. Their displayed lower and upper values were R type-8 25th and 75th percentiles: descriptive interquartile ranges, not confidence or credible intervals and not cluster-adjusted inference. Separate object-level nuclear regressions used ordinary least squares; image-blocked five-fold cross-validation evaluated prediction but did not alter the final model's object-level independence assumption. Regression limits from this analysis were treated as descriptive conditional summaries, not inferential uncertainty across independent images, wells, experiments, or biological replicates. Physical area conversion and clustered uncertainty would require missing acquisition metadata and a new statistical analysis.

### Direct Trajectory Features

Direct trajectory summaries were computed from the assembled count and calibrated-glucose data. Available field-level object calls were summed within each physical well and time as described above, and total cells were defined as live plus dead. For direct count features, the physical-well rows were then reduced to one condition-by-time trajectory: at each time, the live, dead, and total counts were the respective medians across retained physical wells within the same experiment, ploidy state, and starting-glucose condition. Thus, the direct-feature unit was this condition-level median trajectory, not an individual physical-well trajectory. Glucose assay readings were summarized separately from imaging-well counts. Within each assay sample and time, glucose was reconstructed from each retained luminescence reading as the nonnegative value `(Lum - b)/(aD)`, where `D` is the dimensionless model multiplier defined above rather than the recorded dilution factor `X`. The assay glucose summary was the median of uncensored reconstructed readings when any were available and otherwise the median of all reconstructed readings, with censoring retained as a flag.

Condition-level live/dead growth features used smoothing splines on log1p-transformed nonnegative median count trajectories with smoothing parameter 0.55. If fewer than four unique time points were available or spline fitting failed, finite-difference extrema were used. Early exponential growth was the slope from a linear regression of log1p median live count on time from the first observation through the first observed time at which the condition trajectory reached 70% of its maximum observed median live count.

Live-cell area under the curve (AUC) was computed separately for each experiment, ploidy state, and starting-glucose condition by trapezoidal integration of its median-across-physical-wells live-count trajectory between the first and last glucose-measurement times. Net total-cell gain to glucose end was the maximum median total-cell count in that window minus the median total-cell count at window start, using the nearest trajectory observation at or after the start. Display and fixed-yield summaries floored this value at zero. The fixed starting-glucose-1 summary filtered to condition-level trajectories at 1 mM, calculated the within-line-and-ploidy median, and compared elevated and baseline states. This quantity is an unnormalized total-cell increment, including cells produced and later observed as dead; it is not a live-cell AUC, a glucose-normalized yield, or a size-normalized yield.

Robust derivative summaries used a separate smoothing rule. Log1p live-cell trajectories were smoothed with parameter 0.62 and evaluated on 260 time points. The outer 6% of the evaluated time range was excluded, and the feature was the 90th percentile of the remaining finite first derivatives. If spline fitting failed, derivatives were calculated from interpolated smooth values by finite differences.

Line- and ploidy-level features were assembled from the condition-level trajectory summaries. Low-glucose growth was the median robust derivative across experiment-by-starting-glucose conditions at or below 0.25 mM. High-glucose exponential growth was the median early log-live-cell slope from condition trajectories at 5 and 25 mM starting glucose. The glucose-free live-cell AUC feature used the condition-level trapezoidal AUC after filtering to 0 mM starting glucose. A separate low-glucose AUC regression fitted condition-level live-cell AUC against log1p starting glucose for conditions at or below 1 mM; its intercept summarized low-glucose live-cell burden.

Glucose use per live-cell time was estimated from a distinct regression. Glucose drawdown was the calibrated concentration at the start minus that at the end of the same glucose-measurement window used for live-cell AUC. After restricting to finite starting glucose, AUC, and drawdown values at starting-glucose conditions at or below 1, we fitted an intercept-bearing ordinary least-squares regression separately for each cell-line-by-ploidy group:

`glucose drawdown = intercept + beta * live-cell AUC`.

The fitted slope `beta` has units of glucose concentration per live-cell-hour. It was estimated directly with glucose drawdown as the response and was not obtained by numerically inverting an AUC-on-glucose coefficient. Because culture volume and a concentration-to-amount conversion were not included, the slope was not interpreted as an absolute amount per cell-hour. These small within-group regressions were descriptive, did not pool slopes hierarchically, and did not propagate calibration or count uncertainty.

For direct-feature comparisons, elevated-minus-baseline differences were divided by the corresponding within-line change in the scalar ploidy metric. The primary comparison used the four-group scope. The five-group comparison added SUM-159-fuse as a scope sensitivity analysis. Final displayed effects were divided, feature by feature, by the across-line sample standard deviation without recentering. If that denominator was unavailable or nonpositive, the median absolute effect and then 1 were used as fallbacks; values were clipped to the display range of -3 to 3. These features were descriptive summaries of observed trajectories and were not treated as a single omnibus measure of starvation robustness.

### Mechanistic Model Specification

Mechanistic analyses fitted a fixed family of 12 hierarchical ordinary differential equation models to live-cell counts and extracellular-glucose observations. Models varied in the number of resource states and pathway fluxes, whether allocation was constrained, and whether a latent waste state was absent, acted through multiplicative growth inhibition, or acted through additive death. The complete structure set was:

| Resources | Pathways | Waste states | Allocation | Waste action | `k`, four lines | `k`, five lines |
|---:|---:|---:|---|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1 | 0 | Not applicable | None | 20 | 24 |
| 1 | 1 | 1 | Not applicable | Multiplicative inhibition | 25 | 30 |
| 1 | 1 | 1 | Not applicable | Additive death | 25 | 30 |
| 2 | 1 | 0 | One pathway | None | 35 | 42 |
| 2 | 1 | 1 | One pathway | Multiplicative inhibition | 45 | 54 |
| 2 | 1 | 1 | One pathway | Additive death | 45 | 54 |
| 2 | 2 | 0 | Flexible | None | 45 | 54 |
| 2 | 2 | 0 | Locked | None | 35 | 42 |
| 2 | 2 | 1 | Flexible | Multiplicative inhibition | 55 | 66 |
| 2 | 2 | 1 | Flexible | Additive death | 55 | 66 |
| 2 | 2 | 1 | Locked | Multiplicative inhibition | 45 | 54 |
| 2 | 2 | 1 | Locked | Additive death | 45 | 54 |

For each well, the state vector contained live-cell number `N`, one or more resource states `R`, and optional waste states `W`. The first resource represented measured glucose. Additional resources were latent and initialized to 1, and waste states were latent and initialized to 0. Initial cell number was modeled on the log scale around 500 cells with a group-specific offset and lower-bounded during numerical solution. Initial glucose was estimated rather than assumed known exactly, with bounds from zero to twice the maximum nominal starting concentration and a prior centered on the nominal well value.

For a model with `R` resources, `P` pathways, and `W` waste states, the number of active mechanistic slots was `L_eff = 3R + WR + 1 + I(flexible allocation)(P - 1)R`: maximum uptake, half-saturation, and yield for each resource; active off-diagonal allocation terms in flexible structures; waste-production terms when present; and maintenance. The operational mechanistic complexity count was `k = L_eff(n_lines + 1)`, counting each active slot once per cell line and once for the shared ploidy effect. It excluded initial-count effects, initial-glucose values, count-noise parameters, Student-t degrees of freedom, and other likelihood nuisance parameters and was therefore not a total count of fitted Stan parameters. Resource uptake followed saturating kinetics,

`u_i = ae_i R_i/(ah_i + R_i)`,

where `ae_i` is maximum uptake and `ah_i` is the half-saturation parameter. Yield-weighted uptake was mapped to pathway fluxes as `Phi = A(Y_R * u)`. The allocation matrix had `P` rows and `R` columns. In each resource column, row `min(resource index, P)` was anchored at one before normalization. Flexible models estimated the other entries; first-resource off-diagonal entries were transformed as `q/(1 + q)`, whereas other active off-diagonal entries used their positive mapped values directly. Each column was then normalized to sum to one. Locked models masked off-diagonal entries to zero before normalization.

Pathway fluxes were combined by the differentiable smooth minimum

`mu_growth = -log_sum_exp(-10 * Phi)/10`,

which approximated limitation by the least-satisfied pathway. Waste-enabled models accumulated latent waste from resource uptake. Depending on model structure, waste acted either through multiplicative inhibition `1/(1 + sum(W))` or through additive death `sum(W)`. Net growth was the inhibited pathway-growth term minus maintenance and any additive waste-associated death. The state equations were `dN/dt = gN`, `dR_i/dt = -u_iN`, and, when waste was present, `dW/dt = KuN`. Maintenance was active in every model. Additional resource and waste states were not assigned experimentally identified biochemical identities.

The count likelihood used a Student-t model for log-transformed live-cell counts. Retained physical-well counts remained separate likelihood observations against the shared trajectory for their experiment, ploidy state, and starting-glucose condition; within-well dependence over time was not modeled. For each observation, `x = log(observed live cells + 1)` was evaluated around `log(predicted live cells + 1)`, with predicted counts lower-bounded for numerical stability and count scale `sigma_N = exp(log(0.2) + 0.5 raw_sigma_N)`. The implemented target contribution was the corresponding Student-t log density minus `x`. Glucose observations were modeled on the luminescence scale. For predicted glucose `G`, expected luminescence was `max(aGD + b, 10^-12)`, where `D` is the dimensionless model multiplier rather than the recorded factor `X`. Uncensored luminescence contributed lognormal density terms, and observations flagged below the limit contributed the corresponding lognormal cumulative probability at luminescence 20. Dead-cell observations were retained for direct summaries but were not used in the mechanistic-model likelihood.

For condition-specific comparison, per-well log likelihood was averaged separately for each of the five retained models within each cell line, ploidy state, and starting-glucose condition. Observation-likelihood loss per well was then defined locally as `max(0, -2 * [model log likelihood per well - best log likelihood per well among the five retained models])`. Zero denoted the best local value, and larger values denoted greater relative loss. This derived observation-likelihood loss was distinct from the optimized-target score `T` and was not a likelihood deviance; the comparison used the operational five-model set.

Positive physical parameters were reconstructed as `exp(log(center) + scale * raw deviation)`. Centers and raw-deviation scales were `6 x 10^-5` and 0.5 for maximum uptake, 1 and 0.5 for half-saturation, 2,500 and 0.5 for resource yield, 1 and 0.5 for allocation terms, 0.01 and 1.5 for either waste-production mechanism, and 0.05 and 0.2 for maintenance. For each well, the raw deviation combined a line-specific effect and a shared ploidy effect multiplied by that well's scalar ploidy metric. Raw line effects, ploidy effects, initial-count offsets, and noise parameters had standard-normal priors before transformation. Initial count was `exp(log(500) + raw_N0)`. The absolute and relative initial-glucose prior scales were `exp(log(0.01) + raw_sigma_base)` and `exp(log(0.05) + 0.2 raw_sigma_rel)`, respectively. The Student-t degrees of freedom had a gamma prior with shape 2 and rate 0.1 and was bounded below by 2. Other physical parameters were positive but had no explicit upper bounds; initial glucose was bounded between zero and twice the maximum nominal starting concentration. Structural masks were applied after parameter reconstruction so inactive entries were exactly zero.

### Optimization, Posterior Inference, And Model Selection

Optimization used CmdStan BFGS with random initial values, Jacobian adjustment, and saved latent dynamics. Larger optimized Stan target values were better. The Stan model used a backward-differentiation-formula ODE solver with relative and absolute tolerances of `10^-6` and a maximum of 50,000 integration steps; no maximum step size was specified. Integration began at time zero. Live cells were initialized at no less than `10^-6`, resources at no less than zero, and waste at zero. State values, predicted counts and glucose, and calibration means were lower-clamped during derivative or likelihood evaluation for numerical stability; these clamps were not fit-exclusion thresholds.

For the five-group optimization set, each of the 12 structures was run from 500 starts, for 6,000 starts in total. An optimization error aborted that start. Stored optimization output could retain a nonzero CmdStan return when readable point estimates were available, but a start was eligible for model comparison only when its objective was finite and its return code was zero. For each structure, the best fit was the eligible start with the largest optimized target, and absence of any eligible start was an error. The near-best count included only eligible starts ending within one optimized-target unit of that best start; the optimization-stability fraction divided this count by all 500 expected starts. Primary four-group full, transfer, and null configurations likewise used 500 starts for each enabled model and data-set row.

For model comparison, the optimized-target score `T` was defined as `-2` times the best optimized Stan target. Because the target included priors and Jacobian adjustment, `T` was not a likelihood deviance. A complexity-penalized optimized-target score was defined as `T + 2k`, where `k` was the operational mechanistic complexity count above, and its delta was measured from the minimum among the 12 structures. These scores were not interpreted as conventional deviance or AIC, and `k` was not presented as a full parameter count. Because optimized posterior-density ordinates depend on priors, Jacobian terms, parameterization, and model dimension, this score does not have the cross-model interpretation of a likelihood, marginal likelihood, predictive score, or posterior model probability. It defined an arbitrary operational filter for retaining a structurally diverse five-model set, hereafter the operational five-model set, not inferential evidence that one structure was true, preferred, or robust. A scientifically validated cross-model selection criterion would require an additional analysis. The Pareto front used lower `k` and lower `T` as its two objectives. Models were ordered by increasing `k` and `T` and retained when `T` equaled the cumulative minimum; ties in `T` were assigned to the lower-`k` model. The resulting operational five-model set was used for downstream posterior summaries and fitted simulations. Unless stated otherwise, all downstream five-model summaries are conditional on this operational set, do not propagate model-selection uncertainty, and do not establish robustness across scientifically justified alternatives. The complexity-penalized score was reported for ranking but was not a Pareto-front axis.

One representative model from the operational five-model set was used for focused overlays and example simulations. Candidate models had to belong to the operational Pareto front, exclude the one-resource/no-waste structure, have finite fit-quality and rank-sum values, pass the NUTS QC gates, receive retrospective human designation as visually acceptable, and satisfy at least one of three criteria: delta complexity-penalized optimized-target score at most 1,000, positive mean transfer-minus-null held-out gain, or automated fit-quality rank at most 3. Automated fit quality was the sum of live-cell root-mean-square error on the `log1p` scale and glucose root-mean-square error divided by the observed glucose standard deviation, lower-bounded at `10^-8`. Transfer gain was the mean, across available line-and-direction cases, of held-out log likelihood under the transfer fit minus that under its matched null.

Eligible models were ranked separately by smaller delta complexity-penalized score, smaller fit-quality score, and larger transfer gain, using minimum ranks for ties and leaving missing ranks undefined. The three ranks were summed without weights; missing rank sums were excluded. The smallest rank sum was selected, with smaller delta score as the next ordering key. No final rule was specified for an exact tie on both quantities. The visual decision and the threshold and rank construction were recorded after fit, transfer, QC, and overlay results were available; the procedure was therefore a mixed quantitative and retrospective subjective selection, not a prespecified or fully objective rule. The operational five-model set, rather than the representative model, defined the downstream posterior and simulation summaries.

**Representative-model selection limitation:** The visual-acceptability criterion was retrospective, and no terminal deterministic rule was specified for an exact tie after rank sum and the complexity-penalized score. The representative-model choice is therefore partly subjective and should not be interpreted as prospective or fully deterministic.

Posterior sampling of the primary four-group and five-group fits in the operational five-model set used four NUTS chains for each of the five retained structures, with 500 warmup and 1,000 configured post-warmup iterations per chain, a dense mass matrix, target acceptance probability 0.99, and maximum tree depth 12. Diagnostic flags were R-hat greater than 1.01, bulk effective sample size below 400, or tail effective sample size below 400; sampling QC additionally required complete chains. Sampling or output errors stopped a run; there was no retry, per-well fallback, or draw exclusion based on diagnostics or ODE warnings.

Posterior draws were reduced to four positive apparent parameters: per-cell yield, apparent glucose uptake, glucose response scale, and maintenance/death. These fitted quantities were not treated as direct biochemical measurements. Within each line and model, high-versus-low effects were defined as `log2(parameter_high/parameter_low)` and summarized by posterior medians and 95% intervals. Across-model summaries used medians, interquartile ranges or outer interval spans as specified, and counts of model-specific intervals above, below, or overlapping zero. As defined above, these across-model summaries describe only the operational five-model set.

### Posterior Parameter Geometry And Size Context

The primary posterior-geometry analysis used the four-line scope and the operational five-model set. For each model, 400 evenly spaced posterior draws were selected. Apparent parameters were reconstructed at the encoded low- and high-ploidy states of each line. Posterior-median values were log2 transformed, yielding one four-parameter point per model, line, and ploidy state. Each parameter was standardized once across all such points by subtracting its global mean and dividing by its sample standard deviation.

Classical metric multidimensional scaling, also termed principal coordinates analysis, was applied to Euclidean distances among the globally standardized four-parameter points, retaining two coordinates without an additive correction. The proportion of positive-eigenvalue variation represented by each axis was reported. Projection quality was assessed with Kruskal stress-1, the correlation between original and embedded distances, and relative distance error. Axis signs and rotations were treated as arbitrary. Low-to-high arrows connected posterior-median ploidy states within each model and line. Each model centroid was the arithmetic mean of its line-by-ploidy points. Correlations between standardized parameter values and the two axes were used only as parameter-orientation aids, not as regression coefficients or uncertainty estimates.

Posterior uncertainty in each ploidy displacement was propagated through the fixed ordination. For every posterior draw, the log2 high-minus-low difference for each parameter was divided by the fixed global scale derived from posterior medians and mapped through the fixed linear projection. An 80% ellipse was formed from the covariance matrix of these two-dimensional displacement draws by scaling its eigenvectors and square-root eigenvalues by the square root of the 0.80 quantile of a chi-square distribution with 2 degrees of freedom. Ellipses were centered on the displayed posterior-median high-ploidy endpoint. They quantify within-model uncertainty in displacement conditional on the selected model, data, scaling, and projection; they do not include uncertainty in absolute low-ploidy position, model selection, model weights, or between-model choice.

Within the same operational five-model set, exact unprojected summaries accompanied the lossy two-dimensional display. For each model, a line profile concatenated the low- and high-ploidy values of all four standardized parameters into an eight-dimensional vector. Pairwise line distance was the root-mean-square difference between these vectors and was summarized across models by the median and interquartile range. Within-line ploidy distance was the root-mean-square of the four standardized high-minus-low components. Model-centroid distance first averaged standardized parameter values across all line and ploidy points within each model and then calculated four-dimensional root-mean-square distance between model centroids. Parameter decomposition retained each signed standardized high-minus-low component; these components were not normalized shares and were not constrained to sum to one.

Size-context analyses were restricted to primary-scope per-cell yield and apparent-uptake effects within the operational five-model set. The adjusted-area-filled effect added the log2 elevated-to-baseline adjusted median-area ratio to the per-cell parameter effect; lower and upper area bounds were added to the corresponding posterior bounds. The volume-proxy-filled effect added the log2 latent-volume-proxy ratio. Because the volume proxy had no interval, its point estimate was added to every posterior bound and its uncertainty was not propagated. Across-model summaries used medians and interquartile ranges of model-specific medians, and directional support classified each model's 95% interval as positive, negative, or overlapping zero. These transforms supplied size context rather than direct biochemical uptake or volumetric-yield measurements.

### Prediction, Transfer, And Mechanistic Sensitivity Analyses

Five-group fitted-trajectory atlases within the operational five-model set selected the maximum successful optimized draw for each of the five retained structures and simulated every five-group well from 0 to 152 h at 0.5-h intervals. Observed live-cell counts and glucose were compared with the corresponding modeled `N` and first-resource trajectories. Additional resource and waste trajectories remained model-implied. Values above 1.5 times the within-line observed maximum were suppressed only in the display; raw simulated values were retained.

Growth-surface predictions within the operational five-model set used 100 posterior draws for each of the five retained structures. Instantaneous high-minus-low net-growth differences were reconstructed over 40 log-spaced glucose values from 0.0001 to 25 and 40 values of the second latent resource from 0 to 1. For each line, model, and grid cell, the 100 draw-level contrasts were reduced to a posterior median. The fraction-positive summary was then the fraction of the five model-specific posterior medians greater than zero. Thus, posterior draws determined the within-model median, whereas the displayed fraction summarized sign agreement only within the operational five-model set; it was not a posterior probability. Phase-space simulations used the best five-group optimization draw for each retained structure containing a second resource. Low- and high-ploidy trajectories were simulated for starting-glucose conditions 0.25, 1, and 5 on a 1-h grid, and every fifth adjacent time segment was retained for directional display. One-resource models were excluded from this phase-space analysis.

### Learned-Environment Prediction-Transfer Benchmark

The learned-environment benchmark used all five matched cell-line groups and the operational five-model set. Each target line contributed two reciprocal splits. In the low-to-high split, the complete higher-ploidy target state was withheld while the lower-ploidy target state remained available; the high-to-low split reversed those roles. Each training data set therefore contained nine of the 10 line-by-ploidy states: both states from the four non-target lines and one state from the target line. The resulting design comprised 50 fitted tasks from five models, five target lines, and two directions. The denominator counts states rather than equally weighted observations.

Each 9-of-10 transfer fit was obtained from 500 optimization starts. Starts were eligible when their log probability was finite and the optimizer return code was zero, and the eligible start with the largest log probability was retained. The matched 10-of-10 comparator fits were selected by the same rule. Optimization used CmdStanR with random initialization; no explicit seed was supplied, and the realized generated seeds were not retained. A more specific optimizer algorithm and any nondefault tolerance settings were not established for these benchmark fits.

For each fitted task, we searched for a constant environment under two modes. The common-R1 mode searched 161 values of the first resource, spanning 0 to 25 mM glucose, while fixing the second resource at 1. The model-native mode used the same R1-only search for one-resource models and crossed the 161-value R1 glucose grid with 101 second-resource values spanning 0 to 1 for two-resource models. Waste was initialized at zero. `R1` used the established glucose concentration scale in mM. `R2` was latent; neither a physical concentration unit nor a biological identity was established for it.

Candidate environments were evaluated with the 9-of-10 fit. Feasibility required nonnegative predicted instantaneous net growth for both states of each of the four complete training lines and for the observed state of the target line. For the favor-high objective, each complete-line contrast was high minus low; the favor-low objective reversed the sign. The selected environment maximized the worst requested-direction contrast across the four complete lines. Ties were resolved deterministically by the mean requested-direction contrast, minimum predicted growth, and then ascending first- and second-resource coordinates. The withheld target state was not used to select the environment.

After selection, the environment was frozen and the low- and high-ploidy instantaneous net-growth contrast for the target line was evaluated under both the 9-of-10 transfer fit and its matched 10-of-10 full fit. The primary reference was the high-minus-low contrast from the target-inclusive 10-of-10 fit. This comparator includes the state withheld from environment selection and is therefore a fitted-model reference, not an independent prospective test against an observed competition outcome. Exact end-to-end success required both a strictly positive worst requested-direction contrast across the four complete training lines and the requested sign in the 10-of-10 target-line reference. Robust success applied an absolute instantaneous-rate margin of 0.05/144 per hour to both requirements; reference contrasts within plus or minus this margin were treated as neutral.

Success counts and fractions were summarized directly over the fixed task grid. Each search-mode-by-objective summary had 50 tasks, and model-family and target-line summaries had 10 tasks each. These combinations were not treated as independent experimental replicates, and the fractions were descriptive rather than inferential; no confidence or uncertainty intervals were assigned. Internal consistency checks covered state identities and reciprocal masks, exact nine-state training composition, complete fitted-task and environment-search grids, viable selected environments, transfer- and full-fit lineage, selected-start rules, derived contrasts, success flags, and aggregation denominators. These checks establish computational consistency, not empirical or prospective validation.

Transfer/null stress tests were conducted in the four-group scope for all 12 structures and both transfer directions, yielding 96 model-line-direction comparisons. Within a target line, the transfer fit retained the scalar ploidy metric and withheld one ploidy state, whereas the matched null fit used the same split after setting the ploidy metric to zero. Held-out log likelihood summed contributions from live-cell count and glucose-luminescence observations, including censored glucose contributions, and was not normalized by observation count. The score was held-out log likelihood under the transfer fit minus that under the matched null fit, so positive values favored transfer. Saved null and transfer curves were also compared with a target-inclusive four-line full-data reference for MCF10A low-to-high and SNU668 high-to-low cases. The full-data reference was not a split-specific refit and was used only as a fitted comparison; it was not an independent prospective validation target.

Within the operational five-model set, scope sensitivity compared primary four-line posterior effects with five-group effects using the validated mapping for the same five retained structures and four apparent parameters. The five-group analysis reconstructed 400 evenly selected draws per model. Leave-one-line posterior sensitivity used five omission contexts. Four omission contexts were refitted on validated inputs. The SUM-159-fuse omission reused the existing four-group fit because that line was absent from the fitted data. The four- versus five-group comparison reused fitted posteriors and was a scope sensitivity analysis rather than external validation. Leave-one-line summaries describe sensitivity to refitted or reused omission contexts and do not constitute prospective prediction. These analyses used four chains, 1,000 warmup iterations, 1,500 retained iterations per chain, target acceptance probability 0.995, and maximum tree depth 14. QC required R-hat at most 1.01, bulk and tail effective sample sizes of at least 400, zero divergences, and less than 1% maximum-tree-depth saturation per chain.

### Fitted Mixed-State Selection Simulations

Selection analyses were fitted mixed-state simulations under glucose schedules, not empirical mixed-culture competition assays. For each model-line task, low- and high-ploidy parameters were reconstructed from one best successful global optimization fit and propagated jointly through the ordinary differential equation model. The primary simulations used the four-line scope and the operational five-model set. Five-group simulations using the validated mapping used the same model set and the validated all-line input. Parameter uncertainty was not propagated in these best-fit simulations.

The strategy grid contained three-stage schedules. Day 0 selected one of the seven nominal glucose concentrations. Days 2 and 4 independently selected either carry-forward medium or refresh to one of those seven concentrations, giving `7 x 8 x 8 = 448` schedules per task. Each schedule comprised three 48-h segments, for 144 h total. Standard endpoints were output every 1 h and integrated with LSODA. Dense selected examples were rerun at 0.5-h resolution without refitting.

The simulated state contained low- and high-ploidy live-cell populations, shared measured glucose, and any model-specific latent resource and waste states. The initial high-ploidy fraction was 0.5. Initial total live cells equaled the mean of fitted low- and high-ploidy initial-cell estimates for that model-line task. At refresh, glucose was set to the scheduled value, extra resources were reset to 1, wastes to 0, the current high/low fraction was preserved, and total live cells were rescaled to the task's seed total. The protocol therefore represented repeated reseeding/refresh cycles rather than uninterrupted culture expansion.

Let `N0` be the line-specific seeded total and `p0 = 0.5` the initial high-ploidy fraction. At 144 h, the high- and low-ploidy fold changes were `F_H = N_high(144)/max(N0 p0, 1e-8)` and `F_L = N_low(144)/max[N0(1-p0), 1e-8]`. Refresh events reset total abundance to `N0` while preserving mixture fraction, but the fold-change denominators remained the original day-0 abundances. “Expansion by at least one-fold” meant `F_H >= 1` for favor-high or `F_L >= 1` for favor-low: final abundance at least equaled day-0 abundance, rather than doubling.

The directional endpoint was `L = log[(N_high(144) + 1e-8)/(N_low(144) + 1e-8)]`; the raw favor-high score was `S = L`, and the raw favor-low score was `S = -L`. A viability-qualified score equaled `S` when the corresponding fold change was at least 1 and was missing otherwise. For the reported selections, a missing qualified score was replaced row by row with `S`, so every finite endpoint was ranked by the raw directional score alone. Final total live cells did not serve as a secondary key in the reported ranking. Exact score ties retained the first schedule in the exhaustive grid, ordered by ascending day-4 action index, then day-2 index, then day-0 index. The modal schedule across lines was the most frequent schedule; frequency ties selected the lexicographically smallest schedule code. These rules define the reported selections without establishing that the effective raw-score fallback was the scientifically intended estimand.

**Selection-simulation estimand limitation:** The reported ranking effectively used unconstrained directional enrichment because non-expanding schedules fell back to the raw score. The expansion indicator and deterministic tie rules are defined above, but it remains unresolved whether viability was intended to exclude non-expanding schedules or whether final total live cells was intended to act as a secondary ranking key. The reported selections were described as generated and were not reranked.

Dense primary-model examples used the modal objective-specific schedules selected across the four primary lines for the retrospectively chosen representative from the operational five-model set. Displayed residual glucose was bounded below by zero and displayed high-ploidy fraction was bounded between zero and one; these guards did not alter saved endpoints or ranking.

Across-line summaries within the operational five-model set used the arithmetic mean of line-specific rank-1 day-6 log ratios: four line contexts for the primary scope and five for the five-group scope. Vertical ranges were the empirical 10th and 90th percentiles of those line-specific rank-1 endpoints. They represent cell-line heterogeneity, not standard errors, confidence intervals, credible intervals, or posterior uncertainty. Objective success fraction was the fraction of line-specific rank-1 endpoints with the intended sign: positive for the favor-high objective and negative for the favor-low objective.

Initial-condition sensitivity used the primary four-line global scope and a separate four-model subset with complete grid support. Initial total cells were 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, 1,600, or 3,200, crossed with starting high-ploidy fractions from 0.1 through 0.9. Changes in strategy and endpoints were summarized relative to a 500-cell, 50:50 baseline. This sensitivity subset was selected from, but distinct from, the operational five-model set; its results also required complete-grid availability.

Leave-one-line selection sensitivity used two different operations. In the primary scope, saved simulations supported the operational five-model set, two objectives, and four omitted-line contexts. In the five-group scope, the analysis selected the best schedule for each existing model-line-objective task and recomputed aggregate summaries after omitting each line. The latter analysis neither refitted the mechanistic models nor reran simulations; it was a descriptive omission sensitivity of existing line-specific fitted-simulation endpoints, not independent validation. Both omission summaries retain the operational-set limitation defined above. For each omission, summaries included the objective-favoring mean score, mean final high-ploidy fraction, mean total live cells, success fraction, and modal schedule. Omission values were compared with the corresponding all-line aggregate, and displayed ranges spanned the all-line and omission summaries.

### Code, Data, And Reproducibility

Analysis and model code is publicly viewable at [https://github.com/Richard-Beck/GlucoseStarvation](https://github.com/Richard-Beck/GlucoseStarvation). The public repository state available for this analysis was not a tagged release or an immutable snapshot of the complete analyzed state. The repository had no release, archive DOI, license, citation file, pinned R or Python environment, container, or complete record of external model-weight versions.

Raw microscopy images and glucose workbooks, processed count and glucose data, model-ready inputs, the serialized classifier, fitted optimization outputs, posterior draws, selection-simulation outputs, and figure-reproduction tables were not present in the public repository, and no public data archive, accession, restriction statement, or request procedure was identified. Exact end-to-end reproduction is further limited by missing microscopy acquisition metadata and physical pixel calibration, manual glucose-workbook curation without a row-level executable map, incomplete production classifier training and execution records, and an unfrozen software environment. The five-group selection simulations condition on one best optimization fit, and their omission summaries aggregate saved line-specific results rather than leave-one-line model refits.

**Code, environment, and data-availability limitation:** A tagged or archived code release, code and data licenses, citation metadata, frozen software environments and external model weights, and stable locations for raw data, processed data, fitted outputs, and figure inputs with access conditions were not available. The public code reference is not a complete release or archive of the analyzed state; end-to-end reproduction and publication-grade deposition therefore remain incomplete.

## Figure S14. Learned environments transfer requested ploidy direction in a model-dependent manner
(a) Schematic of the learned-environment benchmark. For each reciprocal target-line split, one of 10 cell-line/ploidy states was excluded from fitting and environment selection. The remaining nine states comprised both ploidy states from four complete lines and the observed state from the target line; the complete lines defined the requested high- or low-ploidy objective, and the observed target state contributed to the viability constraint. The selected constant R1/R2 environment was frozen and the requested sign was then assessed using the target-inclusive 10-of-10 fitted reference. R1 is the first resource passed as glucose, whereas R2 is a model-implied second resource. The reference is fitted-model output, not an independently measured competition outcome.

(b) Requested-sign fractions across the fixed set of 50 fitted splits (five retained model families × five target lines × two holdout directions) for common-R1 and model-native environment searches and objectives favoring high or low ploidy. Orange denotes favor-high and blue denotes favor-low objectives. Filled circles show the exact criterion and open circles show the robust criterion; labels give successes/50. Exact success required a positive worst requested-direction contrast across the four complete training lines and the requested sign in the 10-of-10 target-line reference. Robust success applied an absolute instantaneous-rate margin of 0.05/144 per hour to both requirements. For favor-high objectives, common-R1 search succeeded in 8/50 splits under both criteria and model-native search in 35/50. For favor-low objectives, common-R1 search succeeded in 15/50 exactly and 10/50 robustly, whereas model-native search succeeded in 17/50 exactly and 12/50 robustly.

(c) Exact requested-sign fractions grouped by model family and target line. Columns compare common-R1 search, which fixes R2 = 1, with model-native search, which uses the same R1-only grid for one-resource models and searches the R1 × R2 grid for two-resource models. Cell labels give successes/10 and fill encodes the corresponding fraction. Model-family cells pool five target lines and two directions; target-line cells pool five model families and two directions. Model-native gains for the favor-high objective are concentrated in the two-resource model families but appear across all five target lines; favor-low fractions differ little between search modes. The displayed fixed-task fractions are descriptive, are not treated as independent experimental replicates, and have no confidence or uncertainty intervals. The benchmark therefore assesses sign retention in fitted models and does not establish prospective empirical prediction or a universal ploidy-by-environment rule.
