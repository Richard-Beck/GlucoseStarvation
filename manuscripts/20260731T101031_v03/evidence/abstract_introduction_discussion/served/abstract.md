## Abstract

Whole-genome doubling is common in cancer, but the resource conditions that favor high- versus low-ploidy cells remain unclear. We studied five matched lower- and higher-ploidy pairs from four cellular backgrounds using time-resolved cell and glucose measurements, mechanistic models, and mixed-population simulations. When no glucose was supplied, high-ploidy cultures maintained more viable cells over time in every pair. In four primary pairs, they also depleted more glucose after accounting for how many viable cells were present and for how long, while producing fewer additional cells under low glucose. Improved survival during starvation therefore coincided with greater resource use and lower cell-number output. Across the models retained for inference, high ploidy consistently increased apparent glucose uptake and lowered the fitted maintenance/death term, whereas estimates of yield depended on model structure. Model-derived ploidy effects transferred poorly between cellular contexts. In simulations that placed lower- and higher-ploidy populations in the same environment, progressive glucose depletion favored high ploidy, whereas repeated glucose replenishment favored low ploidy. The strength of this reversal varied with cell line, model, starting density, and mixture composition. These results identify a consistent ecological theme: high ploidy supports survival through resource depletion, while sustained glucose availability shifts the modeled advantage toward low ploidy. Ploidy changes the balance between enduring scarcity and exploiting abundance.
