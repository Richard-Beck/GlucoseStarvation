## Discussion

Across the experimental and modeling results, one consistent ecological theme emerged: high ploidy improved survival as glucose was exhausted, whereas sustained glucose supply favored low ploidy in the fitted competition models. The experimental data define the starvation side of this pattern. Without supplied glucose, high-ploidy cultures maintained more viable cells over time in every matched pair. In four primary pairs, they also depleted more glucose after accounting for how many viable cells were present and for how long, and they produced fewer additional cells under low glucose. The fitted models connected these observations through higher apparent uptake and a lower maintenance/death term. Mixed-population simulations then combined these differences across the full trajectories: progressive depletion favored high ploidy, while repeated replenishment favored low ploidy.

This theme depends on keeping survival, resource use, and cell production separate. High-ploidy cultures maintained viable cells throughout glucose withdrawal, but they did not use glucose sparingly. The glucose-depletion analysis measures how much extracellular glucose disappeared relative to the number of viable cells present over time. It is a population-level measure and does not resolve transport or metabolism in individual cells. Even at that level, the result is informative: in the four primary pairs, greater glucose use was accompanied by lower cell-number output under low glucose. Rapid resource use and efficient conversion into new cells can therefore move in opposite directions, as predicted by rate–yield and cellular-economics theory [16,17]. Cancer glycolysis also supports biosynthesis and redox control as well as ATP production, so higher glucose consumption need not produce proportionally more cells [8].

Related studies support the environmental dependence of ploidy fitness, although the direction differs among systems. WGD mammary-tumor cells have shown slower growth in standard culture and an advantage under low serum, and defined aneuploid cells can gain stress-specific advantages [13,15]. Nutrient-sparse models have linked polyploid population behavior to combinations of resource-use and movement traits [12]. By contrast, lower ploidy improved starvation survival in a glioblastoma model [14]. The present panel adds a consistent high-ploidy starvation phenotype across five matched comparisons to a literature in which the direction varies across systems. The most likely sources of variation are cellular background, the route used to generate the high-ploidy state, the duration and composition of the stress, and the trait used to score survival [25]. Our design cannot separate those factors completely because the five high-ploidy states arose through different derivation routes and most backgrounds were represented by a single derivation.

Cell size contributed to the phenotype but did not account for it. Most high-ploidy states had larger cells and nuclei, as expected after WGD, whereas the fusion-derived SUM-159 comparison retained the ploidy difference without a larger high-ploidy cell. Similar decoupling has been observed after WGD, and both proteome abundance and mitochondrial function scale nonlinearly with ploidy or cell size [5–7]. These relationships matter for interpretation. Fewer cells do not necessarily mean less biomass, and a per-cell uptake parameter cannot be converted into a per-volume flux by multiplying it by area. Direct measurements of dry mass, cell volume, and glucose carbon fate are needed to determine whether high ploidy reduces biomass yield or only cell-number yield.

The most stable fitted changes—higher apparent uptake and a lower maintenance/death term—provide a useful mechanistic starting point. They link the two direct observations: greater glucose depletion and better survival during withdrawal. Several known starvation programs could lower death pressure without lowering glucose use. AMPK can preserve NADPH during energy stress, and mTORC1 suppression can reprogram translation and fatty-acid metabolism to limit reactive oxygen species during glucose starvation [18,19]. Polyploid tumor cells can also carry altered oxidative stress and antioxidant responses [20]. These mechanisms are experimentally testable in the present system through time-resolved measurements of AMPK, mTORC1–4EBP signaling, autophagy, NADPH, mitochondrial function, and reactive oxygen species. The fitted parameter itself does not identify which pathway is responsible.

Glucose-only models did not describe all trajectory shapes as closely as richer structures. Models containing an additional resource or an accumulated environmental effect often fit better. The most informative next step is to measure the medium before assigning biochemical names to the latent states. Extracellular protein scavenging can supply amino acids when free nutrients are scarce [21,22], making protein-derived nutrients one candidate for the additional resource. Lactate and acidity are candidates for an accumulated environmental effect, but they have different biological roles: acidity can alter tumor behavior, whereas lactate can be consumed as a carbon source [23,24]. Metabolomics, isotope tracing, pH measurements, conditioned-medium exchange, and targeted supplementation would distinguish these possibilities.

Cellular background dominated the fitted parameter landscape, and ploidy effects transferred poorly to arbitrary held-out states. The common pattern therefore lies at the level of resource schedule, while the fitted parameter changes remain background specific. Cancer cell lines differ widely in their response to glucose limitation, including their capacity to increase oxidative metabolism [10], and chromosome-state phenotypes are strongly shaped by lineage and genetic context [25]. A targeted search identified selected two-resource environments in which the requested direction carried across fits, especially for high-ploidy-favoring conditions. Because the target was evaluated against a target-inclusive fitted reference rather than new competition data, this benchmark does not establish prospective transfer. Its main value is experimental design: it supplies model-derived environments for direct testing.

The mixed-population simulations provide the ecological synthesis. Progressive depletion favored high ploidy because the fitted high-ploidy populations retained viability as the shared resource collapsed. Repeated replenishment favored low ploidy because continued supply allowed differences in population growth across the full trajectories to dominate. This abundance prediction does not follow from the starvation result alone; it emerges from the fitted growth, resource-use, and death dynamics together. Starting density and initial mixture composition changed the outcome because they changed how quickly the shared environment was depleted. Such frequency- and density-dependent selection is expected in evolving tumors, where resource supply and local crowding vary across space and time [26,27].

The simulations now define a focused competition experiment. Lower- and higher-ploidy cells should be labeled and mixed at several starting ratios and densities, then exposed to the exact depletion and replenishment schedules used in the model. The decisive readout is the change in ploidy fraction alongside total population size, glucose, and cell death. That experiment would test both halves of the ecological theme and reveal whether interactions in mixed culture alter the monoculture-derived prediction. Spatial versions of the assay would be especially informative because glucose, oxygen, pH, and cell density can interact to change selection across tumor-like niches [27].

These short-term tradeoffs may also help explain why WGD persists despite proliferative costs. WGD can buffer deleterious genomic changes and accelerate copy-number evolution, while creating dependencies in mitosis, DNA replication, and proteostasis [3,4,28]. A high-ploidy clone could therefore lose during sustained growth, survive a period of resource collapse, and retain long-term evolutionary opportunities. Resource history may be one of the forces that determines when those genomic benefits become accessible.

The main uncertainties are experimentally tractable. The current comparisons do not isolate ploidy from derivation history across the full panel. Glucose measurements quantify extracellular concentration loss; they do not measure absolute uptake or carbon fate. Cell counts do not measure biomass. The retained model set was selected with an operational score, and mixed-population outcomes were simulated from monoculture fits without propagating parameter uncertainty. Biological replication and production-pipeline provenance were also incompletely documented, limiting population-level inference and exact reproduction. These limits point directly to the next measurements: matched independently generated ploidy pairs, absolute flux and isotope tracing, dry mass and volume, direct co-culture competition, and medium metabolomics. Together, those experiments would establish which parts of the observed tradeoff are caused by ploidy itself and which arise from the cellular state in which ploidy is embedded.

The study identifies a consistent ecological theme: high ploidy supports survival through glucose depletion, while the fitted competition models predict that sustained glucose supply favors low ploidy. The reversal arises because survival, resource use, and cell output are separable traits. In nutrient landscapes that fluctuate across time and space, the fitness effect of ploidy will depend on which of those traits the environment rewards.
