## Introduction

Whole-genome doubling (WGD) is a common event in cancer evolution. It has been inferred in roughly one third of tumors in pan-cancer cohorts and often occurs early, with marked differences among lineages [1,2]. Cells that tolerate WGD can continue to missegregate chromosomes and accumulate copy-number alterations, while the doubled genome can buffer some deleterious losses [3,4]. These effects give WGD immediate cellular costs and long-term evolutionary consequences.

The physical consequences of WGD immediately create a resource problem. WGD often increases cell and nuclear size, although both can be remodeled after the doubling event [5]. A larger cell must synthesize and maintain more material, yet proteome abundance and mitochondrial function do not scale in simple proportion to ploidy or cell volume [6,7]. High ploidy may therefore change resource demand, growth efficiency, and stress tolerance in ways that cannot be inferred from DNA content or size alone.

Glucose is a useful nutrient for testing these consequences because it is central to cancer-cell growth and can become limiting in tumors [8–10]. It supplies ATP, biosynthetic precursors, and reducing power required for proliferation and stress control [8]. Direct measurements in pancreatic tumors have found glucose depletion relative to plasma, and cancer cell lines differ greatly in their ability to survive glucose limitation [9,10]. Prolonged glucose scarcity can also select for clones that grow better under deprivation [11]. Glucose availability therefore connects cell physiology to clonal selection: it can reveal whether the greater material burden of high ploidy is offset by an enhanced ability to survive resource exhaustion.

Prior studies show that nutrient stress can favor either higher or lower ploidy, depending on the system. Imaging and modeling of polyploid cancer populations showed that behavior in nutrient-poor environments depended on the combination of resource-use and movement traits [12]. In a matched mammary-tumor model, WGD slowed growth in standard culture but improved clonogenic performance under low serum [13]. In a glioblastoma model, lower-ploidy cells were more resistant to starvation-induced death [14]. Defined trisomic cells likewise gained advantages under selected stresses, but the favored karyotype changed with the condition [15]. The direction of the effect therefore depends on lineage, chromosome state, and the resource challenge being imposed.

The unresolved question is how these condition-specific outcomes are assembled from distinct cellular traits. Survival during starvation, glucose use, and production of new cells are separate quantities. A culture can deplete glucose rapidly yet convert less of it into new cells; another can grow efficiently while supply is maintained but lose viability after the resource is exhausted. Rate–yield theory and cellular-economics models show that resource capture, conversion efficiency, and competitive success can favor different phenotypes under different supply regimes [16,17]. Measuring endpoint growth alone therefore misses the ecological tradeoff that can make the same ploidy state advantageous in one environment and disadvantageous in another.

We addressed this question with five matched lower- and higher-ploidy pairs from four cellular backgrounds. Time-resolved microscopy and extracellular-glucose measurements quantified how many viable cells remained, how many cells were produced, and how the shared glucose pool changed over time. Consumer–resource models tested whether glucose alone could explain the full set of cell and glucose trajectories and identified fitted changes that recurred across cell lines and model structures. We then simulated mixed lower- and higher-ploidy populations under depleting and replenished glucose schedules. This final step asked how the measured and fitted differences would combine when both ploidy states competed in the same environment.

Across the study, one consistent ecological theme emerged. When glucose was absent, high-ploidy cultures maintained more viable cells over time in every matched pair. In four primary pairs, high ploidy also produced greater glucose depletion after accounting for how many viable cells were present and for how long, together with fewer additional cells under low glucose. Models assigned high ploidy higher apparent uptake and a lower maintenance/death term, while yield remained sensitive to model structure. In mixed-population simulations, progressive depletion favored high ploidy and repeated glucose replenishment favored low ploidy. Together, these results show that ploidy changes the balance between surviving resource exhaustion and producing cells under sustained supply.
